Does atheism have answers? (Part 3)

Very often the view that God is the cause of the existence of living things on Earth sounds naive, outdated and unscientific. But what answer does an atheistic view of the world have to give to the question of how life appeared?

The theory of abiogenesis is well known: according to it, life can arise from non-living chemical substances. The simple observation of insects or worms appearing in decaying organic matter (rotten meat, for example) seemed to support this view until the 19th century, when in 1861 Louis Pasteur, with his famous experiments, proved that such creatures do not appear by spontaneous generation but from eggs laid there earlier by small organisms, and formulated the principle of biogenesis: all life comes from life.

To deal with the problem of how the first living cell came about, the Russian biochemist Alexander Oparin proposed in 1924 that life arose by chance from chemical reactions in the warm primitive oceans (the “primordial soup”), assuming that the atmosphere must have been rich in ammonia, methane and hydrogen. Sources of energy, such as heat from volcanoes and lightning, acted on carbon compounds, turning them into more complex ones. In an attempt to confirm this theory, Stanley Miller carried out a series of experiments in 1953, which produced certain amino acids – which, however, could never spontaneously form proteins, the building blocks of cells. Later repetitions of such experiments consistently confirmed this dead end.

Alternatively, it is proposed that life could have appeared on Earth coming from other planets. But this view does not solve the general problem of the appearance of life; it simply moves it elsewhere. In reality there are far more problems here, because the carrier of some kind of microbial life (a meteorite, for example) would find it practically impossible to keep its precious cargo alive through the extremely hostile conditions of a journey through space and an impact on another planet. The idea that life came from space, even with the involvement of some intelligent beings, lacks any evidence, as does the existence of life anywhere else in the universe.

So we find that the atheistic view of the world cannot answer the question of how life appeared. On the other hand, can faith in a Creator God be supported scientifically, or do the latest findings overturn it? Molecular biology is revealing more and more the complexity of the cell’s machinery, to the point that the spontaneous creation of even the simplest cell – which, it is generally agreed, is more complex than a spacecraft – becomes impossible. From another angle, cosmology is finding more and more how finely “tuned” the universe is: the balance of the fundamental forces and the cosmological parameters, from the structure of elementary particles to the conditions at planetary and galactic level, led the eminent British astronomers and writers M. Rees and J. Gribbin to put forward the view that the universe is “tailor-made for man”. And Sir Fred Hoyle, who coined the term “Big Bang”, admits: “Nothing has shaken my atheism as much as this discovery.”

So the convincing evidence for one view, against the absence of any evidence for the other, is obvious. Life comes only from life:

“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. […] All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made. In him was life; and the life was the light of men.” (John 1:1, 3, 4)
