Anger and how to manage it

“Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: and be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.” (Ephesians 4:31–32)

These verses seem completely foreign to the reality of modern society. The person of the 21st century has a great many reasons to be constantly angry with something or someone, so much so that he does not hesitate to express the rage he feels at every opportunity.

The main causes of this phenomenon are easy to identify:

  • the modern way of life, full of difficulties, demands, lack of time and other factors that cause psychological pressure and stress.
  • the emotional isolation that many people experience, especially young people, so that anger is often an attempt to attract attention.
  • information overload, which is not only tiring but consists overwhelmingly of bad news (wars, crises, injustices), so that anger builds up.
  • the promotion of rage as a forceful response, something seen especially on social media: “fiery” posts create an illusion of strong character, are widely applauded by readers, and hold up the angry person as a social model of someone dynamic.

In brief, the biological mechanism of anger is as follows: when the brain perceives a threat or a provocation, the amygdala is activated. This is the part of the brain that immediately triggers certain bodily reactions by releasing hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, which prepare the body for an immediate response by raising the heart rate and blood pressure, dilating the pupils for greater alertness, and so on.

When anger is triggered suddenly, the brain has no time to think, because the amygdala, for the sake of a quicker response, bypasses the prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for rational thought; so the bodily reaction is instinctive. Here, though, we must stress that in spite of this mechanism a person does not become the unconscious plaything of his anger, so as to be free of responsibility for what he does in a state of rage. Before the “explosion” reaches its peak, he realises what is happening to him, and he has the choice either to exercise self-control or to give in to the tempting results of the dominance he can exert over others through his angry reaction.

In many places the Bible draws our attention to managing anger, “For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.” (James 1:20).

What, then, does the Bible propose?

  • “Wherefore laying aside all malice, and all guile, and hypocrisies, and envies, and all evil speakings, as newborn babes, desire the sincere milk of the word, that ye may grow thereby: if so be ye have tasted that the Lord is gracious.” (1 Peter 2:1–3)
  • “And every man that hath this hope [of Christ’s coming] in him purifieth himself, even as he is pure.” (1 John 3:3)

We see that at least two clear motives are given as a counterweight, so that a person sets about restraining himself: tasting (that is, lived experience) and the expectation of Christ’s coming. In both cases, the realisation that something higher is at stake weakens the desire for the possible benefits of giving in to the lure of an angry reaction, and shapes new, different and heavenly expectations in a person’s inner world.

Furthermore:

“But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance” (Galatians 5:22–23)

That is, being filled with the Holy Spirit brings forth in a person’s heart the very things that are the real medicine against every kind of anger and wrath.

So in a world torn apart by rage and conflict, let us seek peace and the transformation of our character in the “more excellent way” that the Word of God sets before us.
