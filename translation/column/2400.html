The humbling of Nebuchadnezzar: myth or medical possibility?

We are in Babylon in the 6th century BC, at the height of the Babylonian empire under the rule of Nebuchadnezzar, at a time when the main wars of expansion had been brought to a successful end. The great monarch had gathered abundant wealth, had built a magnificent capital, and believed he could spend the rest of his life in comfort and happiness.

At exactly this point God shows Nebuchadnezzar a dream that shook him, because it foretold an extremely humiliating turn in the life of the arrogant monarch. In this dream Nebuchadnezzar was likened to a tall tree, with rich foliage and fruit, which was suddenly cut down by divine command (the event is told in the 4th chapter of the book of Daniel).

The prophet Daniel, who served at the king’s court, interpreted the dream, foretelling a period of “seven times” during which Nebuchadnezzar would lose his reason and behave like an animal: he would feed on grass, live out in the open exposed to the weather, and keep company with the wild beasts.

“they shall drive thee from men, and thy dwelling shall be with the beasts of the field, and they shall make thee to eat grass as oxen, and they shall wet thee with the dew of heaven, and seven times shall pass over thee, till thou know that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will” (Daniel 4:25)

Someone might claim that such a thing could happen only in a myth. Today, however, we know that the state of madness into which Nebuchadnezzar fell is described medically as boanthropy (from the Greek for ox + man). It is a neuropsychological disorder in which a person believes himself to be a bovine animal, and behaves accordingly.

Boanthropy is a form of clinical zoanthropy, a wider category of rare delusional disorders in which patients perceive themselves as an animal.

  • In brief, the clinical signs of boanthropy are as follows:
  • The person walks on the ground on all fours.
  • He stops speaking and makes lowing sounds.
  • He develops a preference for foods such as grass and hay (“they shall make thee to eat grass as oxen”).
  • He may join a herd of cattle to wander and graze (“thy dwelling shall be with the beasts of the field”).

(More information: https://www.icliniq.com/articles/emotional-and-mental-health/boanthropy)

At this point we must stress that it would be wrong to attribute the cases of boanthropy known today to the same cause that brought this about in Nebuchadnezzar.

Medically, the cause of boanthropy is not clear; it is thought that it may be a different aspect of other neuropsychological disorders, such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.

In the case of Nebuchadnezzar, however, it is clear that the cause of what happened to him, and of his recovery, was divine intervention.

What is remarkable, though, is that the biblical description presents with astonishing accuracy the clinical symptoms of boanthropy that modern medicine also records. With the help of science, then, we can know with certainty today that this passage of the Bible does not belong to the realm of imagination, but closely matches the recorded symptoms of modern medicine.

In the end, Nebuchadnezzar’s humbling and his repentance resulted not only in his being restored to his kingdom with even greater majesty, but also in a declaration, from the lips of such a man, that touches all of us to this day:

“…all whose (God’s) works are truth, and his ways judgment: and those that walk in pride he is able to abase.” (Daniel 4:37)
