The archaeological and historical reliability of the New Testament (Part 2)

The New Testament is the most reliable book of antiquity. Archaeological finds and historical records agree on this and confirm it – records that examine the New Testament no longer as a theological text but as a historical text with theological content.

The four Gospels are in complete harmony and agreement with one another; so, without overlooking the reliability of all the Gospels, we will look in more detail at the Gospel of Luke.

The evangelist Luke was born in Antioch, lived in the 1st century AD, practised as a physician and was a fellow worker of the apostle Paul. He is the author of the Gospel that bears his name and of the book of the Acts of the Apostles, which he wrote after thorough study and questioning of the eyewitnesses of the life of Jesus Christ and the Apostles, and also from events at which he himself was present.

In Luke’s two books we find references to:

Names of Roman emperors

Names of Roman governors and officials

Names of leading members of the Jewish priesthood

Local offices of the time (for example, politarchs)

Historical events with great impact on the world of the time (persecutions, famines)

Accurate medical and nautical knowledge

32 countries, 54 cities and 9 islands

Almost all of these references have been accurately confirmed by archaeological finds and historical sources. This shows Luke’s intention to record events exactly as they happened, with a care that would mark a great historian.


Some archaeological finds for the New Testament:

Ancient Capernaum: discovered in 1838; in 1866 the ruins of the synagogue were identified.

The pool of Bethesda: discovered in 1956, it has five porches, as stated in the Gospel of John 5:2.

The Pilate inscription: in 1961 an inscription in Latin naming Pontius Pilate was discovered at Caesarea.

Lysanias: in a temple in Damascus the only record was found of Lysanias, tetrarch of Abilene, who is mentioned in Luke 3:1.

The “politarchs”: in 1962 inscriptions were published that mention this office (in the KJV, “the rulers of the city”), which appears nowhere in ancient Greek literature but only in Acts 17:6, 8.

The Erastus inscription: discovered in 1929 in Corinth, it shows that Erastus really was the city’s treasurer, as Paul says in Romans 16:23.

The theatre of Ephesus: where the rioters gathered (Acts 19:23–41), has also been excavated, and inscriptions have been found that stress the city’s worship of the goddess Artemis (Diana).

The Messianic prophecies:

There are more than 300 prophecies in the Old Testament foretelling the coming of the Messiah. In the New Testament we see all these prophecies fulfilled in one person, Jesus Christ. It is not very difficult, even for an ordinary student, to make some calculations to estimate the probability of each of these prophecies being fulfilled. Indeed, formal studies at a Christian college, for just 20 Messianic prophecies, give a probability of their being fulfilled in a single person of 1 in 10175!

All this evidence, of course, comes as a supplement to the overall reliability of the Bible, which a person perceives and experiences when he comes to know Jesus Christ and tastes the works of God in his life.
