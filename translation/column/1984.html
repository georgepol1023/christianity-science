“All these are the beginning of sorrows” (Matthew 24:8) – Part 2

In the corresponding passages in the synoptic Gospels of Matthew and Mark, the Lord Jesus Christ uses the Greek word ōdines – birth pains, translated “sorrows” in the King James Version – in the plural, to describe the sequence of world events before the end (the end of the times of the Gentiles and the beginning of the seven years in which God will deal with the people of Israel as a nation).

So we read: “Take heed that no man deceive you. For many shall come in my name, saying, I am Christ; and shall deceive many. And ye shall hear of wars and rumours of wars: see that ye be not troubled: for all these things must come to pass, but the end is not yet. For nation shall rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom: and there shall be famines, and pestilences, and earthquakes, in divers places. All these are the beginning of sorrows.” (Matthew 24:4–8). “For nation shall rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom: and there shall be earthquakes in divers places, and there shall be famines and troubles: these are the beginnings of sorrows.” (Mark 13:7–8).

The Lord often used expressions from people’s everyday life to convey deeper meanings. With this in mind, let us look a little more closely at childbirth.

As the start of labour draws near, the activity of the womb increases. This increased activity takes the form of contractions of gradually greater strength and frequency, but not yet regular, which are called preparatory contractions. Their purpose is to prepare the womb for the birth: “…all these things must come to pass, but the end is not yet.”

The mark of real labour is birth pains: the involuntary, regular, painful contractions of the womb that lead to the birth. They are rhythmic, and in between they ease off completely until the next one. The shorter the interval between them, the more we understand that the birth is near. A moment will come when we have the last birth pain… We think there will be more, but this will be the last. We do not know it. So we must always be ready to meet the Lord our God.

The amazing thing is that THE WORD OF GOD points out this very situation (yes, the Lord is coming, but we still have a little time) and records it.

In 1 Thessalonians 5:1–3 we read: “But of the times and the seasons, brethren, ye have no need that I write unto you. For yourselves know perfectly that the day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night. For when they shall say, Peace and safety; then sudden destruction cometh upon them, as travail upon a woman with child; and they shall not escape.” And in the critical text, in the ancient Greek in which the Gospel was written, we read: “…as the birth pain (ōdin) upon a woman with child; and they shall not escape.”

P.S. The Greek nation has much to give the world, but we think that by far the most important is the Greek language, unquestionably the richest language in the world. The Holy Spirit used the Greek language to give people the Gospel – the heavenly message of God’s love for man! So we distinguish ōdini (birth pain) from the similar-sounding odyni (spelt with o and y), which is mainly pain of the soul. We often use the phrase “anguish of soul” (psychiki odyni) to describe distress or grief of the soul.

“And it came to pass, that the beggar died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom: the rich man also died, and was buried; and in hell he lift up his eyes, being in torments, and seeth Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom. And he cried and said, Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, and cool my tongue; for I am tormented in this flame. But Abraham said, Son, remember that thou in thy lifetime receivedst thy good things, and likewise Lazarus evil things: but now he is comforted, and thou art tormented (in Greek, odynasai).” (Luke 16:22–25).
