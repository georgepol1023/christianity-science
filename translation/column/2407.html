The acute fear response and the reliability of the biblical account

In this month’s article we travel to Babylon in 539 BC, to the last night before the Babylonian empire was overthrown by the sudden invasion of the Medes and Persians. Royal power was then held by Belshazzar, probably a grandson of Nebuchadnezzar: a man marked by great arrogance and by contempt for the true God, even though he knew how God had humbled his predecessor (see Daniel chapter 4).

His ungodliness reached its height that evening when, as he feasted with his officials, they began to praise false gods and idols, drinking wine from the golden sacred vessels that had been taken from the temple of God when the Babylonians invaded Jerusalem 47 years earlier.

At that very hour a man’s hand appeared and wrote a mysterious inscription on the wall (the well-known “MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN”), which Daniel later deciphered, and which foretold the coming of God’s wrath and the end of Belshazzar’s kingdom. The whole event is told in the 5th chapter of the book of Daniel.

Belshazzar’s reaction to the supernatural sight of the hand writing on the wall is very interesting:

“Then the king’s countenance was changed, and his thoughts troubled him, so that the joints of his loins were loosed, and his knees smote one against another.” (Daniel 5:6)

This verse accurately describes an acute fear response, with a sense of helplessness in the face of a threat, as it is known today at the biological and neurophysiological level:

  • the king’s countenance was changed: The Aramaic text says that his brightness changed, meaning that his face went pale. In a state of fear this happens because of the adrenaline that is released, which constricts the blood vessels of the skin so that more blood is directed to the muscles and other organs.
  • his thoughts troubled him: Fear stimulates the area of the brain called the amygdala, which largely bypasses the prefrontal cortex (responsible for rational thought) so that the body’s automatic reactions are triggered at once. The result is mental disorganisation and an inability to process the event rationally.
  • the joints of his loins were loosed: When a person feels completely helpless in the face of a threat, he does not react with “fight or flight” but with a third kind of response, “freezing”. This description of Belshazzar points to a “freeze” response with elements of collapse, caused by involuntary muscle relaxation that leads to a loss of support and of bodily control.

  • his knees smote one against another: This is uncontrollable trembling of the legs. It is caused by the release of adrenaline to tense the muscles for the “fight or flight” response.

We can see that the text mentions elements of both the “freeze” response and the “fight or flight” responses. This is because these responses often do not appear on their own but overlap, or alternate very rapidly in the body.

This is exactly what shows how realistic this verse’s description of Belshazzar’s reaction is.

When an incident is fiction, one would expect the account of such a fear response to leave out the seemingly contradictory signs that confirm how realistic the description is. The story tends to be more one-dimensional (for example, only terror, or only paralysis, or only flight), with the ultimate aim of underlining the moral lesson of what happened to the “wicked” king.

Here, on the contrary, the writer does not set out to explain why Belshazzar was afraid, or to interpret his reaction; he simply records it. This is very strong evidence that we are dealing with an eyewitness account and not a made-up story.

We see, then, that even in a single verse, the way the events are recorded reveals the reliability of the biblical text, and strengthens our certainty that in studying the Bible we come into contact with the recorded truth of the Word of God.
