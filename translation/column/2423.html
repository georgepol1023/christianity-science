The Artemis II space mission

From 1 to 11 April 2026 a crewed space flight around the Moon took place, named Artemis II. It was the first crewed flight of its kind since 1972 (the Apollo 17 mission). This mission is part of a wider programme called Artemis, whose aim is to return people to the Moon, after the first historic Moon landing of Apollo 11 in 1969 and the last one in 1972 (Apollo 17).

The Artemis II flight was not meant to land on the Moon. It was a test mission to confirm that the systems and procedures for the spacecraft and crew work correctly, as part of the preparation for the Artemis III and Artemis IV missions, the latter intended to be the first crewed lunar landing since Apollo 11.

The planning of the Artemis programme goes back to the early 2010s, and for more than a decade after that it was in the stage of designing and developing the necessary systems, facing great technical challenges and significant delays. The uncrewed Artemis I mission was launched only in 2022, while in 2023–2025 the preparation of Artemis II entered its final phase, and it finally took place in April 2026.

The cost of this space flight exceeded 4 billion dollars, while the total cost of the programme so far is more than 90 billion dollars.

All these years of preparation and the high cost show how difficult it is to make a journey into space: it needs special technologies to be developed, precise calculations, and very sensitive parameters to be set for the survival and safety of the crew. The conditions that sustain life, which we take for granted on planet Earth, are absent outside it, and highly specialised procedures and technology are needed to reproduce them inside spacecraft.

In earlier articles we referred to the anthropic principle: the finding that a large number of parameters must lie within specific numerical limits for life on Earth to be possible. By 2009, 322 such parameters had been identified (from the mass of the proton to the rate of expansion of the universe), and the probability of all of them occurring together has been calculated as 1 in 10304 – something mathematically impossible. So the very existence of a planet in the universe able to support life is a statistical paradox; in other words, it is a miracle!

It is worth mentioning that shortly before the spacecraft passed behind the far side of the Moon, where communication with Earth would be lost for about 40 minutes, one of the four astronauts recited verses 37–40 of chapter 22 of the Gospel of Matthew, sending the message that one of the most important mysteries on Earth is love.

Many astronauts share the experience, and have said so, of needing to express their faith and share spiritual messages because of what they lived through on their journey into space. The best-known example is the reading of a passage from Genesis on the Apollo 8 mission on Christmas Eve 1968. The explanation for such expressions (which belong to a wider phenomenon known as the “overview effect”) is summed up in the words of John Glenn, the first American to orbit the Earth, in 1962:

“To look out at this kind of creation out here and not believe in God is to me impossible.”

We close our article with another famous saying, by James Irwin, the eighth man to walk on the Moon, in 1971:

“Jesus walking on the earth is more important than man walking on the moon.”
