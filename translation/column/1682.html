Evolution or creation? (Part 1)

At the request of the readers of our column, we will return to the subject of the theory of evolution, beginning this month a series of articles in which we will briefly present the reasons why this theory is now collapsing in the light of the latest scientific findings.

The theory of evolution is not an idea first put forward by Charles Darwin in 1859; he was influenced by existing ideas about the gradual evolution of living organisms, which have their roots in ancient Greece. In 1831 Darwin took part in an expedition to chart the coasts of South America, where, working as a naturalist, he studied various kinds of animals which, although alike, showed clear adaptations to their particular environment. So he concluded that the small changes a species shows as adaptations to its environment, inherited over very long periods of time, caused one species to evolve from another. So the huge variety of living organisms we see in nature today came by gradual evolution from a single-celled organism, which came from the random joining together of organic molecules, which in turn came from the random joining together of inorganic molecules in a primordial soup! The mechanism on which evolution rests is natural selection. This means that when a feature appears in a population that favours its survival, the individuals that have that feature survive better and leave more offspring, while the other individuals struggle to survive and gradually die out. This observation is correct.

But beneficial adaptations of living organisms to their environment can never lead to the appearance of a new species (macroevolution); they always take place within the same species (microevolution). As we said, at the root of the theory of evolution lies the hypothesis of abiogenesis, according to which at some point living matter happened to arise from non-living chemicals. This was indeed a common belief among ordinary people until the 19th century, as they saw new living organisms appear in dead, decaying matter (for example, worms in rotten meat).

But in 1861 Louis Pasteur showed by experiment that this happened only from eggs laid earlier in the meat by insects or micro-organisms (omne vivum ex vivo – all life comes from life). To deal with the problem of how the first living cell came about, the Russian biochemist Alexander Oparin proposed in 1924 that life arose by chance from chemical reactions in the warm primitive oceans, assuming that the atmosphere must have been rich in ammonia, methane and hydrogen. Sources of energy, such as heat from volcanoes and lightning, acted on carbon compounds, turning them into more complex ones. In an attempt to confirm this theory, Stanley Miller carried out in 1953 a series of experiments simulating the conditions in which life might have appeared (the primordial soup). The results of these experiments at first caused great excitement at how easily certain amino acids – of which proteins, the building blocks of cells, are made – could be produced.

But it was soon found that there was no way to go further and create any protein, and so none of the more complex molecules that living matter is made of. Today we know clearly the reasons why Miller’s experiments failed, since the conditions on the early Earth are now thought to have been different. Also, the heat sources and the water itself would have favoured breaking amino acids down rather than building them up. Indeed, these experiments showed that together with the amino acids, tar and carboxylic acids are also produced, which would chemically poison any primitive form of life. Mathematically, too, if all the chemical processes required were left to happen completely at random, even under the most favourable conditions possible, the probability of creating a single living molecule is of the order of 1 in 1060! Today, of course, we know that the process of making amino acids, then proteins and so cells, is not random or blind, but is guided with wonderful precision by the DNA molecule, with which, God willing, we will continue our study in our next feature.
