Addiction to and dependence on “smartphones” (Part 3)

“I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly.” (John 10:10)

As we saw in our two previous features (July and August), addiction to and dependence on so-called “smart” phones and the internet are real. In the United Kingdom, for example, the average age at which children get their own phone is 7, while 13-year-olds are already attending recovery programmes. Recent research in Greece too has shown that 78% of children aged 5–12 use the internet, rising to 90% among children aged 10–12. But apart from “healthy and balanced use” of the internet and phones, children (and adults) become addicted to these media: they play games until dawn, live in isolation, shut themselves away, have no friends, and show insecurity and fears.

“When I use my phone I feel euphoric. That’s why I use it as much as I can. It’s rarely out of my hand,” says an 18-year-old girl, scoring another point for those who argue that “smartphones may prove to be the drug of the 21st century”.

Is there a way to check how healthy (or not) your relationship with your phone is? The specialists of the renowned Cleveland Clinic in the USA recommend answering the following questions:

Has your phone use begun to affect your work, or does it stop you concentrating on your lessons or your tasks?

Do you think about your phone even when you are not using it?

Do you feel impatient and irritable when you are not holding your phone?

Do you check your phone constantly, and for longer than you would like?

Have members of your family started complaining that you spend too much time on your phone?

If you answer yes to one or more of these questions, you are in the danger zone, and you need to take steps to limit your phone use. We must stress that taking steps early is very effective. A walk in the countryside, taking part in sports and pleasant conversation with loved ones are incredibly effective in getting us unstuck from dependence.

As for the mechanism behind it, many studies have shown that using our phone gives us pleasure, as it is directly linked with the chemical in our brain called dopamine, the “pleasure neurotransmitter”. Dopamine can indeed be released in our brain every time we find a new piece of information on our phone, or when someone contacts us on it. So it is no wonder that we all want to be on our smartphones, and a lot. Here we must stress that the human brain is unquestionably the most complex organ in the universe, and we know very little about it. But there is Someone who knows everything and truly loves us: the Triune God!

We should point out that almost every kind of dependence “takes its victim captive” in a similar way. In the hypothalamus (a small part of the brain), and scattered through the brain, is the system of reward and punishment. Stimulating the punishment centre (to deter a dependence) does affect our behaviour, but the effect is short and weak. Indeed, despite the gruesome pictures on cigarette packets, consumption has fallen only slightly. This is also why the urging and advice of parents and teachers have little effect in setting a person free. The reward system, whose main neurotransmitter is dopamine, has a far greater effect.
