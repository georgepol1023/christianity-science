Lessons from two astronomers who died recently

At the end of this summer we learned of the death of two important figures in science, specifically in astronomy and astrophysics, one of whom was Greek. They are the American Frank Drake and Dionysis Simopoulos, who died on 2 September and 7 August respectively.

Frank Drake (1930–2022) was one of the pioneers of research into the existence of extraterrestrial intelligence. In 1961 he devised the famous Drake equation, which uses 7 astronomical parameters; depending on the values they take, it calculates the number of possible extraterrestrial civilisations in the Galaxy. In the 1960s he led the conversion of the Arecibo observatory into a radio astronomy observatory, and in 1974 he wrote the Arecibo message, which contains basic information about humanity and planet Earth in coded form. It was beamed into the sky from the facility’s radio transmitter as an attempt to communicate with some extraterrestrial civilisation that might receive and decode the message. In 1984, with other astronomers, he founded the SETI Institute (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence), which focuses on searching for signals from extraterrestrial civilisations.

Dionysis Simopoulos (1943–2022) was honorary director of the Eugenides Planetarium in Athens, with a wide range of activity and a long contribution in many areas of astronomy education and of presenting the subject to the public. As a young man he lived for 11 years in America, where he was director of the Louisiana planetarium, and during the 1960s he saw many launches live and followed many space missions. About a year before his death, in an interview, he said: “What we know about the universe is infinitesimally small. We know that it is made up of about 1 trillion galaxies, and each of them consists of 100–200 billion star systems, and all of this that we see makes up 5% of the contents of the universe. […] That is, 95% of the contents of our universe are unknown. So we know almost nothing about the universe.”[1]

Gleaning these points from the wide contribution of these scientists, we reach some very important conclusions. On the one hand, the astonishing progress in observing the universe and in space missions leads us in the end to realise the extent of our ignorance, and our smallness in the face of the sizes and forces that lie just beyond our planet and our solar system. On the other hand, the search for extraterrestrial life and the attempts to communicate with other civilisations show man’s inborn tendency, and his need, to come into contact with something higher – something better that might even prove to be his salvation, especially now that things on our planet are getting worse than ever before.

The Gospel of Christ, however, has good news for people. Realising our ignorance and our smallness, when we accept it in a humble spirit, can be a springboard for getting to know the One who created and rules over matter, energy and all the forces in the universe. That is why He is called “the Lord of hosts” (in Greek, literally “the Lord of powers”).

“The humble shall see this, and be glad: and your heart shall live that seek God.” (Psalm 69:32)

As for the heavenly message and the longed-for visit from heaven, that came to pass 2,000 years ago, when the Son of God, Jesus Christ, took a human body “to seek and to save that which was lost” (Luke 19:10), offering Himself as a sacrifice on the cross of Golgotha. Your own message to heaven needs no special coding, nor does it need to be beamed from the transmitter of a radio telescope, and there is no doubt whether it will reach the One it is sent to. Simply “enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly.” (Matthew 6:6).
