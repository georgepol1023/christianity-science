The bibliographical reliability of the New Testament (Part 4)

With this short article we complete (in brief) the case for the bibliographical reliability of the New Testament as well.

Many writings of the Apostolic Fathers of the first three centuries quote passages of the New Testament word for word. These quotations are so numerous that from them alone the whole New Testament could be reconstructed (apart from 11 verses)! This shows that the New Testament existed in written form from early on (the end of the 1st century), and that there was a conviction that it was a duty to preserve the texts exactly as they had been received from their original authors.

At the Council of Carthage in AD 397 the books that still make up the New Testament today were finally settled: that is, the Canon of the New Testament was formed. The choice of these 27 books was not the result of a meeting of a closed circle of clergy who shaped the canon of the New Testament according to the beliefs or interests of a ruling minority. The choice of the 27 books of the New Testament was the confirmation and ratification of the texts that the church and the Christian community had already accepted and taken to heart. So we are not talking about deciding which books were sacred, but simply about making official the canon of the New Testament. The criteria for choosing these 27 books, which ensured that they were given by inspiration of God, were:

  • That they were written by an apostle, or by a man closely connected with an apostle
  • The spiritual quality of their content
  • Their acceptance by all the Christian churches

It is also worth noting that the texts of the New Testament were translated quite early into many languages, which helped spread them and made the New Testament accessible to ordinary people. The spread and use of the Greek language also played an important part, since it can convey meanings with clarity, precision and relative simplicity.

All this evidence obliges anyone who disputes the reliability of the texts of the New Testament to reject the whole of ancient Greek and Latin literature as well, since the evidence on which its reliability rests is very slight compared with the mass of evidence that confirms the reliability of the New Testament.

So we see that the Lord, in a wonderful way, took care to preserve His written Word through the centuries and to bring it safely into our hands today.

In conclusion, disputing the reliability of the Bible is an expression of irrationality and of sterile contrariness. From archaeology, from the study of historical documents and even from mathematics, there comes a flood of proofs of the historical accuracy and the truth of the text of the Bible. The truth and validity of the text of course include all of God’s miraculous interventions. For the Gospels, as they are written, if they were not true and completely reliable, would be the worst possible recipe for spreading a new faith: written a very short time after the events, they both describe very specific things and call on a great many witnesses (for example, at the raising of the widow’s son at Nain, the day of Pentecost and so on).

The holy God used the Greek language to give people the New Testament – a great honour, but also a heavy responsibility before Him. Through the centuries it was He who safeguarded its complete reliability in every respect. We are called, while it is called To day, to return to “the fountain of living waters”, to the Word of God, to the Lord Jesus Christ (Jeremiah 2:13), and to declare boldly that “neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.” (Acts 4:12)
