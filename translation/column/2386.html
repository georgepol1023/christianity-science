Biomimetics | Learning from the wisdom of creation

“But ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee; and the fowls of the air, and they shall tell thee: or speak to the earth, and it shall teach thee: and the fishes of the sea shall declare unto thee.” (Job 12:7–8)

Biomimetics is the effort to imitate various mechanisms found in nature in order to develop materials and systems that serve mankind. These mechanisms, already “tested” for their efficiency as we watch them working in natural systems, are a source of inspiration for developing equally efficient technologies in many areas of human life.

In earlier articles we mentioned examples in medicine, telecommunications, materials technology, engineering and other fields. In today’s article we will look at some more examples.


Ants and city traffic lights

Traffic engineers in various countries have studied how ants move along their trails. Ants have no police officers or traffic lights, yet they move in an organised way and avoid traffic jams, because they follow “invisible” chemical substances (pheromones) that show which trail is emptier.

This observation has led to smart algorithms for city traffic lights: instead of changing at a fixed interval every minute, the lights can “learn” and adapt to the traffic, just as ants find the quickest route to food.

The movement of flocks of birds and shoals of fish

When we see a flock of birds in the sky or a shoal of fish in the sea, we notice that they all move together in harmony without colliding. This is achieved without any leader or coordinator: each bird or fish simply follows three simple rules:

1. Don’t bump into your neighbour.

2. Try to keep the same speed as those around you.

3. Stay close to the group.

Scientists are experimenting with this idea for smart transport systems: cars connected to one another (with sensors and GPS) can move like a “flock”, keeping steady distances and avoiding the sudden braking that causes traffic jams.

A diabetes drug inspired by a lizard

In the 1980s two researchers in Mexico were studying the extremely slow metabolism of a kind of lizard called the Gila monster, and in the following decade they managed to isolate from its venom a substance they named exendin-4.

This substance is similar in structure to the human hormone GLP-1, which regulates blood sugar but breaks down in the body within only a few minutes, whereas exendin-4 breaks down much more slowly.

This led to the development of exenatide, a synthetic analogue of exendin-4 that has a similar action but lasts longer in the human body, and is used successfully as a medicine for type 2 diabetes. It is therefore a very important application of biomimetics in pharmacology, used in 90% of medicines for diabetes and obesity.

The list of examples we could mention would be truly endless. But the main question about biomimetics should be whether man is setting out to copy something that irrational nature has really produced by chance.

The systems chosen for imitation are not simply happy coincidences that could have arisen from random, blind processes. They are systems optimised for the task they perform, with clear marks of intelligent design, and man cannot fully reproduce them artificially. Such “natural technologies” cannot have arisen as an accumulation of small random changes over millions of years, as the theory of evolution maintains.

Nature “cries out” that there is a Creator, and man, through biomimetics, confirms this reality, even if unintentionally.
