Carnival

=================== ◄ Carnival ► ===================

There is no doubt that we live in a time when science has made astonishing progress. So while one would expect this progress to have brought freedom from superstition, irrational fears and primitive practices, modern man still seems to a great extent bound by such inheritances, which becomes especially noticeable during the period of Carnival.

The word “Carnival” is thought to come from the Latin words “carne” = meat and “vale” = to abstain (or, according to others, from the word “levare” = to lift, remove). The corresponding Greek word is “Apokria” (apo + kreo), which also means “abstaining from meat”, and refers to the period of preparation for the fast of Great Lent, as set by the tradition of the Orthodox Church. This period, also called the “period of the Triodion”, lasts three weeks and ends with “Meat-fare Sunday” (from the next day, “Clean Monday”, Great Lent begins).

But the most usual meaning of the word “Apokria” or “Carnival” today is the great popular festival of people in fancy dress, who have fun in the streets singing, dancing, drinking, staying up all night, teasing other people, telling jokes and crude jests, and in general doing things they would not do at other times.

The origin of all these events and customs of this period is pagan, and is found in ancient Greece and ancient Rome. That is why certain basic features of the corresponding festivals of antiquity have been preserved to this day:

  • The time of year of the celebration: in ancient Athens, at the beginning of March – that is, around the days that today roughly correspond to the Carnival period – as spring arrived the Anthesteria were celebrated, a festival in honour of the god Dionysus.
  • The double face of the festive character: the Anthesteria were on the one hand a festival of flowers, wine and unbridled joy, and on the other a festival of the dead and of souls. Today, during the Carnival period, these two elements also dominate, since on the one hand there are festivities of people in fancy dress, and on the other there is the great Saturday of Souls before Meat-fare Sunday, when, according to the teaching of the Orthodox Church, the living can have communion with the dead and pray for their repose.

    We should make clear here, of course, that the Orthodox Church is not responsible for this double-sided climate, since its teaching is very clearly opposed to carnival festivities. But to restore the truth of the Word of God, we must say that according to Holy Scripture it is not possible for living people to have communion with the dead, nor is there any point in praying for the dead, since the destiny of their souls is judged once and for all, immediately after the moment of their death:

    “And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27)

    Nor does Holy Scripture anywhere show any communication between the dead and the living to be possible.
  • Fancy dress: it already existed in ancient Greece, since during the Dionysian festivals the followers of Dionysus wore animal skins, smeared their faces with the lees of wine (that is, the dark sediment of the must) and generally tried to take the form of a goat-like man, because that is what they believed the Satyrs (demonic deities) who accompanied Dionysus in his revels looked like.
  • Revelry and teasing: behind the disguise a person feels that they shed their own character and the limits of everyday life and step into another role, in which they can give themselves to unbridled revelry, getting drunk and saying whatever they like without restraint. The same happened at the Dionysian festivals, since the Satyrs (whose role those in disguise were playing) were deities whose characteristic marks were immorality and obscenity.
  • Offerings to the dead: the ancient Greeks believed that on the second day of the Anthesteria (called “Choes”) the gates of Hades opened and the dead came up to the upper world, to return there the next day. So many honours were offered to the dead, together with a “panspermia” of grains – a dish made of cereal seeds and pulses, something like today's kollyva (from the pots used to prepare it, the third day of the Anthesteria was called “Chytroi”).
    In our own day there is a similar custom, since according to the teaching of the Orthodox Church every Saturday is dedicated to the dead. But the Saturdays of Souls of the Triodion are considered especially important, and offerings are made to the dead, such as kollyva, prosphora and “psychoudia” (small loaves).
  • Various customs of pagan origin: for example, the wild dancing of people in fancy dress (in antiquity its purpose was to help the earth magically to sprout through the stamping of feet), bonfires at crossroads (lit to drive away the winter, with people jumping over them “for luck”) and so on.

In the time of the Roman Empire, the typical customs of the festival of “Lupercalia” showed many similarities with today's Carnival. It was held in honour of Lupercus, the god of fertility and protector of shepherds. The priests of Lupercus, after making sacrifices in the sacred cave of their god, went round the streets of Rome dressed in the skins of the sacrificed animals, teasing people.

In the Western world, and mainly in the USA, there is a corresponding festival of fancy dress, Halloween, celebrated every year on 31 October (the eve) and 1 November (the day itself). Halloween is like Carnival, since it includes fancy dress and also a remembrance of dead saints and martyrs (the name comes from the phrase “All Hallows Eve” = the eve of [the feast of] all the saints). But Halloween differs from Carnival in that it has a more mystical flavour, both in its fancy dress and because it is linked with occult rites.

The meaning of the mask and of fancy dress:
“The person wearing the mask feels inwardly transformed, having temporarily taken on the properties of the god or demon (note: or, more generally, of the role) represented by the mask” (Biedermann, Hans. “Dictionary of Symbolism”, p. 218).

Here an important side of Carnival, concerning fancy dress, comes to light: wearing the mask and the costume, a person feels that they set aside their own personality, their image in front of others and their role in society, and feel that they have the chance to let loose some other instincts and behaviour that they hesitated to show in normal circumstances. And even if they had no such tendencies, they are encouraged to cultivate them by imitating others.

The excuse is often given that it is good to keep our customs. But these are pagan customs, and ones that degrade a person as a personality.

Another argument is that through carnival festivities people have fun and escape from everyday life. Saint John Chrysostom answers this, saying: “To rejoice in everything is not good... for the adulterer too rejoices when he destroys his neighbour's marriage... so let us not look at whether someone rejoices, but whether he rejoices in something good.”

The way of life Holy Scripture proposes:

“And be not conformed (in the original Greek “μη συσχηματίζεσθε” = do not take on the same shape or form) to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God” (Romans 12:1–2)

The reason people dress up is their attempt to have fun. But after the carnival is over they find that they did not receive the joy they expected, and may even be sad, as they remember the effort and money they spent in vain, and the unseemly behaviour they showed.

But real happiness does not come from decorating the body with special clothes, or from stepping into a different role through a mask and a costume. For what is suffering in a person, so that they are not happy, is not their body but their soul, which longs to fill the emptiness it has. Not knowing this, a person believes that they will stop feeling this sense of emptiness by satisfying the desires of their body. But a non-material being, such as the soul, cannot be satisfied by material provisions and entertainments that concern the body (fancy dress, dancing and so on).

So changing one's outward appearance does no good at all, but rather the opposite, since it distorts a person's image into something other than what God made them. The real transformation of a person comes through the renewing of the mind and the restoring to health of the spiritual man which is the work of the Word of God. For the purpose of the Word of God is not to make a person a will-less being who blindly obeys religious rules, but to guide them into reasonable service (that is, with a sound mind).

Then a person, discerning what the will of God is in their life and doing it, comes into fellowship with their Maker, and so is complete and happy, with no further need to turn to entertainments that wear them down in body and soul.

Such a way of life is not unrealistic or far off, because the gulf between God and man is bridged through Jesus Christ. The salvation, eternal life, completeness and happiness that Christ gives are not earned by human effort but are given freely and by grace to the person who admits their sinfulness, repents and believes in the sacrifice on the Cross and the resurrection of Jesus Christ.
