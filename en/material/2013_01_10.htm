The phenomenon of school violence

School violence means any act between pupils, or against a pupil by someone from outside the school, that aims to inflict some kind of harm, either psychological or physical. School violence takes various forms and shows itself either physically (hitting, pushing, coercion and so on) or verbally (threats, insults, demeaning names, spreading malicious rumours, racist behaviour and so on).

In our time a new form of bullying has also appeared: electronic bullying, or cyber-bullying, which includes sending threatening or abusive material by email, through mobile phone services (for example SMS, MMS) and through social networking sites (for example Facebook). Indeed, online bullying at school age is gradually becoming a scourge, since a large percentage of pupils have unsupervised access to electronic media that offer unlimited ease of networking with very many people, and relative anonymity too – that is, wonderful tools and a wide field of action for bullying and violence.

Research in recent years shows that in Greece the percentage of pupils who have fallen victim to some form of school bullying stands at 14.7%. Although this percentage is clearly better than the corresponding 30% in England and 77% in the USA, it is higher than in countries such as Ireland (13%) or Norway (9%).

As for the reasons why a child may become the target of school bullying and violence, it should be made clear that in most cases the child who is targeted has not done anything wrong that would justify such behaviour towards them. What makes them a “target” is some difference they have, either in their outward features or in their personality. Examples of such features are skin colour, nationality, sex, weight, accent, way of speaking, school performance, shyness and so on. So any child may be targeted, depending on how different they may seem in whatever school environment they find themselves in. Here we can also see the pressure these children are under somehow to “stifle” their difference and conform to the demands of the majority.

On the other hand, the reasons that lead a child to behave aggressively towards others may be:

  • the fact that the child was once, or still is, on the receiving end of violent behaviour (that is, the victim becomes the bully).
  • the need to feel superior to others, or jealousy of another child's better performance (trying to belittle anything superior) in lessons, sport and so on.
  • the desire to attract attention and become popular.
  • a sense of emptiness or unhappiness that drives them to pass this lack of joy on to others too.

But psychologists point out that the deeper cause of bullying behaviour is not the difference of the child who is bullied, but should be sought within the child who does the bullying. If the environment in which this child is brought up pushes them towards feelings of jealousy, insecurity and unhappiness, or is a rejecting or even violent environment lacking empathy (deep communication) and acceptance, then bullying behaviour is more likely, and understandable.

But apart from conditions within the family, the influence of wider social conditions is also considered decisive. This is the subject of the “broken windows” theory, according to which, if some instances of social disorder begin to appear in an area (broken windows in a building, graffiti on walls, litter in the street, noise at quiet hours and so on), if these are left unchecked, they will multiply (for example, some people will not hesitate to break the rest of the building's windows, hence the name of the theory) and may lead to serious forms of delinquent behaviour, even crime. In other words, when citizens see others breaking the rules of social behaviour at no cost, they tend to do the same, and indeed the physical decline of an area through such relatively simple instances of social disorder can lead to other, more serious symptoms, such as theft and vandalism. On the contrary, residents and shopkeepers taking care of the look of an area, and dealing with cases of rule-breaking, prevents not only the escalation but also the repetition of such things.

Returning to school violence, research also points to further factors that encourage such behaviour to develop, such as spending many hours on violent video games, mixing with children who have violent tendencies, and also teachers' lax attitude towards cases of bullying, or their unwillingness to deal with the issue, leaving the field open for incidents of violence to escalate.

On the other hand, the school's handling of such behaviour carries the risk of stigmatising, isolating or even excluding children who bully from the school environment, so that they may react with even more aggressive behaviour. So although such measures may be judged necessary on occasion, they do not correct the problem; they simply move it outside the school, where it becomes a social problem.

So it becomes clear that the place where this issue must be dealt with is the family. Parents must be watchful and carry out parenting diligently, according to the principles we set out in an earlier article. So when there is communication and a loving relationship with the child, parents will be able to know any particular traits of their child's character (whether a potential “bully” or “victim”) and to notice any changes in their behaviour, and so they will be able to handle in the right way any situations that may arise, and also to prevent them.

As for the above, first of all parents need to supervise their children, so as to know the people they spend time with and the activities they are busy with. A much-discussed case is the use of the internet, which opens the way to communication with people who may be strangers, while there is also the danger of personal or family details being shared, which could be used as a means of bullying (for example, blackmail with the threat of making personal details public to others). So supervision by parents is necessary as circumstances require, with suitable discretion, and depending on the age of the children. Indeed, Jesus Christ Himself watches over and intervenes to protect the flock that the heavenly Father has entrusted to Him (John 10; see also Proverbs 27:23).

The cooperation of parents and teachers may also be needed, whether in the case of a child who is a “bully” or a child who is a “victim” of bullying. This cooperation will not aim to repay evil but to stop the bullying and bring to their senses those who do it, but without crushing or stigmatising them. Such right intervention by parents in the school environment shows the child who is a “victim” the practical care of their parents, who besides being comforters also become their defenders.

As for the child who is on the receiving end of bullying, having a friend plays a very important part in support at difficult moments and in resisting the pressure put on them through bullying to conform to the majority so as not to be targeted. Especially in the case of Christian children, we should say that it is the common testimony and experience of most of them that in the difficult school or university environment each of them found themselves in, the Lord sent them one more person who was a Christian, or at least a child with moral principles. A typical example in Holy Scripture is the three faithful friends Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, who, finding themselves in Babylon, an environment hostile to their faith, together took a decision that could cost them their lives, so as not to worship an idol (see Daniel 3).

But we should point out that there are also some protective “walls” that parents can help the child to build, so that on the one hand they are not led into bullying behaviour, and on the other, if they are on the receiving end of such behaviour, they do not collapse psychologically or react wrongly.

One such “wall” is developing healthy (that is, not selfish) self-esteem. This means realising that as a person they matter to their parents and have value, not because of their own ability or performance, but as a creature of God, whom He formed with His own hands and for whose sake He became man and sacrificed Himself. This healthy self-esteem will free the child from the desire to attract attention and from the anxiety to prove their worth, by anxiously chasing high achievement or trying to put others down. They will also understand that other people matter too, as creatures and – potentially – children of God, and so are worthy of respect. Convictions like these prevent the formation of a “bully” character.

On the other hand, understanding that their value is not determined by any difference they may have and that their state of mind should not depend on being accepted by everyone, they learn not to be shaken when they are on the receiving end of bullying, but to deal with it in a healthy way without feeling the pressure to conform to the – usually negative – models around them so as not to be targeted. Equipped with such an outlook and state of mind, they will be able to comfort other children who are victims of bullying, and also to face children who bully calmly, and perhaps even with advice.

But for such a right understanding and healthy way of thinking to be formed and to last, the enlightening of the mind and the cleansing of the heart are essential. These qualities spring from developing a personal relationship with Jesus Christ, and through Him with God the Father. And this relationship is cultivated through a diligent spiritual life and acquiring blessed spiritual habits: study of the Word of God, prayer, being filled with the Holy Spirit, fasting, regular church attendance. Then the instructions of the Word of God and the companionship of His presence are a real spiritual armour that guards a person and gives them understanding so that they have sound thoughts and act rightly as each situation requires and according to the will of God.

“Ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you” (Acts 1:8) “Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand” (Ephesians 6:13).
