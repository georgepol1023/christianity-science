The theory of abiogenesis and Miller's experiment

The theory of abiogenesis, or spontaneous generation, is the cornerstone of the theory of evolution. According to this theory, living matter can arise from non-living chemical components. Although Darwin himself did not deal with abiogenesis but with the evolution of living organisms from the level of the cell upwards, the spontaneous generation of life is a necessary condition for the theory of evolution to stand, because it tries to explain how the cell, the basic structural and functional unit of life, came into existence, so that evolution could then take place.

Ideas about the spontaneous generation of life already existed in ancient times, while the term “abiogenesis” became established in the 19th century and expressed people's belief that new living organisms can arise from dead matter as it decomposes. The many examples from everyday life, such as maggots appearing in rotting meat, were enough to count as unshakeable proof of the spontaneous generation of life.

But in 1861 the theory of abiogenesis was finally overturned by Louis Pasteur, whose experiments proved that the microbes and insects seen on spoiled meat are not born spontaneously from dead matter, but come from eggs laid on the meat earlier by insects or micro-organisms. So the principle of biogenesis was stated: “omne vivum ex vivo”, that is, all life comes from life.

To deal with the problem of how the first living cell came about, the Russian biochemist Alexander Oparin proposed in 1924 that life arose by chance from chemical reactions in the warm primitive oceans, given that the atmosphere must have been rich in ammonia, methane and hydrogen. Sources of energy, such as heat from volcanoes and lightning, acted on carbon compounds, turning them into more complex ones.

In an attempt to verify this theory, in 1953 Stanley Miller carried out a series of experiments using the components that he believed reproduced the conditions of the early Earth where life might have appeared (the primordial soup). In suitable laboratory chambers (vessels) he placed water, methane and ammonia. He boiled the mixture and exposed it to an electric discharge. After a week some amino acids were found in the mixture. This caused great excitement at how easily the amino acids that make up proteins – the building blocks of cells – could be produced. But the excitement soon faded, because from this mixture of “bare” amino acids it was impossible by any means to go on to form a protein, and so none of the other more complex molecules that make up living matter.

Newer scientific evidence has shown that Miller's experiment, and any other similar experiment, could in no way prove that a living cell can be put together from non-living components. Besides, the conditions of that experiment were chosen in order to get the desired result, and not on the basis of estimates of the real conditions on the early Earth.

The most important (and insurmountable) problems faced by Miller's experiment, and by any other experiment of this kind, can be summed up as follows:

  • It is now established that in the atmosphere of the early Earth there was plenty of oxygen, which would have been destructive to the spontaneous synthesis of amino acids. But the possibility that there was no oxygen is just as unfavourable, because a lack of oxygen means an absence of ozone, and so the free passage of ultraviolet radiation, which would destroy any amino acids that formed.
  • Miller's experiment did not form only amino acids. The amino acids were separated (filtered) from the other by-products of the experiment with a suitable amino acid “trap”. But in nature such separation would not be possible, so mixing with the by-products of the chemical processes (tar and carboxylic acids) would poison any primitive form of life.
  • The assumed sources of energy (heat, ultraviolet radiation), and water itself, act to favour the breakdown and not the synthesis of amino acids. That is, under these conditions, the rate at which any amino acids formed would break down would be greater than the rate of their spontaneous synthesis.
  • A necessary condition for making a protein is, first of all, the formation of a polypeptide chain, which happens when amino acids link together. But the natural tendency of amino acids is to react with other organic molecules rather than with each other. So what was it in the primordial soup that, in the absence of the right cellular machinery, pushed random chemical reactions to follow the unexpected direction?
  • Amino acids come in two forms: right-handed and left-handed. Each form is the mirror image of the other. This property is called optical isomerism. Random chemical processes produce right-handed and left-handed amino acids in a ratio of 50% (as expected) for each form. So if life came from random chemical processes, why are only left-handed amino acids found in all living organisms?

So we see that at the very first step of the spontaneous appearance of life there are insurmountable problems in explaining how this process developed blindly and entirely by chance. And at the following steps the problems become even greater, since we must explain why 20 particular kinds of amino acid were chosen in living organisms out of the 80 that exist in total, and how they were placed one beside another in exactly the right order, which led the chain of amino acids (the polypeptide chain) to fold in a unique way so as to form exactly the right three-dimensional shape of the molecule that will form the protein. And, on top of that, we must explain how all this happened for each of the 200 proteins that make up a single cell.

From a mathematical point of view, moreover, if all the chemical processes required were left to take place entirely at random, even under the most favourable possible conditions, the probability of forming a single living molecule is of the order of 1 in 1060! In other words, mathematics makes the possibility of life arising by chance impossible! (For details see the related material in the right-hand column.)

Besides, it is now known that the universe is of finite age; that is, the time it has existed is not infinite, so that these infinitesimally small probabilities might have even a slight hope of being fulfilled. And although the age of the Earth is calculated at about 5 billion years, the period during which conditions are thought to have been favourable for the creation of life is only 400 million years – far too short a time for the probabilities just mentioned to be fulfilled. In any case, even if all the time needed had been available, because of hydrolysis and the processes that break amino acids down in general, the rates of synthesis and breakdown of amino acids are such that there would be the same (constant) number of amino acids in the mixture whether a few minutes or a few million years had passed.

Today, of course, we know that the process of making amino acids, then proteins and so cells, is neither random nor blind but is guided with wonderful precision by the DNA molecule, which contains all the information, systematically arranged, so that these steps are carried out exactly.

The DNA molecule, which we will discuss in our next article, reveals the existence of a creative Mind who designed and brought into being the wonderful phenomenon of life, and who, though He is Himself life, died for our sins. But He rose again for our justification, and gives eternal life to whoever believes in Him. His name is Jesus Christ.
