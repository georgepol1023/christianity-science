The anthropic cosmological principle

The planet Earth is not an insignificant grain of sand drifting through the universe that happens to host life; rather, as much scientific evidence shows, the whole universe is made in such a way that life can exist on Earth. Many 20th-century scientists came to this conclusion, and expressed it as the anthropic cosmological principle.

After the work of Lamarck and Darwin in the 19th century, the (otherwise unproven) ideas that life appeared by chance and that species evolved on Earth won wide acceptance. So the fact that the world was created and is sustained by God was pushed aside, and the view prevailed that everything is the result of chance and mechanical processes. The planet Earth, then, had no reason to be seen as anything more than a grain of sand in the vast universe on which life happened to develop.

But from the first half of the 20th century, astonishing scientific discoveries began to appear that gave a completely different picture. In 1955 the American physicist Robert Dicke argued that without a great number of cosmological coincidences, life in the universe would be impossible. That is, a great number of cosmological parameters must lie within specific numerical limits for life in the universe to be possible.

Later, various physicists found that life could not have appeared if there had been even a small deviation in the size of the so-called universal constants. This led the British astronomer John Barrow and the American physicist Frank Tipler to put forward the anthropic cosmological principle in 1986. The principle holds that the laws, the universal constants and the basic structure of the universe are not chance occurrences, but support a cosmological necessity for the creation of intelligent beings able to observe the universe. In other words, the anthropic principle holds that to meet all the conditions needed to create life with an intelligence capable of observing the world scientifically, a whole universe is needed!

By 2009, 322 cosmological parameters had been found that lie within very narrowly defined numerical limits so as to favour life on Earth, and new ones are constantly being discovered. The probability of all of them coming together by chance has been calculated at 1 in 10304! If we consider that the number of all the planets in the universe is only of the order of 1022, we can see that the probability of the conditions needed for life existing on a planet by chance is 1 in 10282 – that is, mathematically impossible!

These parameters can broadly be grouped into 5 categories:

  • Physical: these concern the numerical value and effect of physical constants, such as the speed of light, the mass of the proton, the relationship between the four fundamental forces and so on.
  • Universal: these concern parameters that apply across the whole universe, such as the age of the universe, its rate of expansion, its uniformity and so on.
  • Stellar: these concern the star of our planetary system, the Sun, and its relationship with other star systems. Such parameters include the age of the Sun, its mass, its colour, the average distance between stars and so on.
  • Planetary: these concern properties of our planet and its relationship with the other heavenly bodies of our solar system. Such parameters include the Earth's period of rotation, its magnetic field, the tilt of its axis, the Earth's planetary shield, its interaction with the Moon and so on.
  • Atmospheric: these concern properties of the Earth's atmosphere: the amounts of the atmospheric gases and the ratios between them.

In the material listed in the right-hand column we describe, as examples, how some parameters from each category have their effect.

Through the anthropic principle, then, we see modern science declaring that life on Earth is not the result of chance but of wise design. The Word of God tells us about the wise Creator and His love, as He showed it to us on the cross of Golgotha, and calls us to know Him. Blessed shall we be if we do.
