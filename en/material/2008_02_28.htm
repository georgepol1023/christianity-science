The reliability of the Old Testament through archaeology

A great many archaeological finds confirm many statements and accounts of the Old Testament. And this concerns not only the most famous events and people, such as Joseph or Moses, but extends to secondary details too, such as the names, genealogies and place names mentioned in many parts of the Old Testament.

This shows the value of including these passages in the text of Holy Scripture: although lists of names, genealogies and places often have no obvious significance for the ordinary reader, for others these passages are worth as much as an archaeological and historical handbook.

Here, then, are some examples of the archaeological finds and historical references that confirm the accuracy of the Old Testament texts.

A reasonable question is whether writing, or ways of writing, existed at the time the first books of the Old Testament were written. We know today that ways of writing already existed in the 4th millennium BC, that is, before the Flood. And in the time of Moses (around 1,300 BC), as the finds show, there was alphabetic writing, and it was in common use even among ordinary people. This answers the claim that the accounts of the Pentateuch come only from oral traditions. Besides, the long lives of the people in the first chapters of Genesis show that there need not have been a great gap in time as the information passed from generation to generation.

The evidence that Moses is the writer of the Pentateuch comes from:

  • The narrative itself, which shows that the writer lived at the time of the events described and was an eyewitness, completely familiar with the geography, plants and animals of the region.
  • The fact that the Pentateuch contains more Hebrew loanwords of Egyptian origin than the rest of Holy Scripture. This is entirely to be expected, since Moses was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians (Acts 7:22). The very name “Moses” is Egyptian and means “drawn out of the water” (Exodus 2:10)
  • The “internal evidence”. The Pentateuch states in many places that Moses is its writer. Other books of the Old Testament refer to this too, while the Gospels and the Epistles speak many times of “the writings of Moses”.

Some of the most important archaeological finds:

  • Ur of the Chaldees: a city for which there was no record at all, until in 1922 the city of Ur came to light in excavations by Leonard Woolley. Together with the historical setting in which the account in Genesis places Abraham, it appears that this is not an imaginary story or an imaginary person.
  • The Ebla tablets: discovered in northern Syria in 1968. They contain the oldest mention of the city of Jerusalem. They also refer to Sodom, Gomorrah and other cities, and contain names from the period that we also meet in the Old Testament. Ur is also mentioned, as a city of Haran.
  • The story of Joseph in Genesis: many Egyptian customs and formalities are described accurately and are confirmed today. A great number of the events that make up the biblical account are historically sound, with astonishing accuracy. Joseph's tomb has also been found.
  • David: there was no archaeological find confirming his existence, until in 1993 an inscription mentioning the name David was found in northern Israel. Later the palace of King David was also discovered.
  • King Solomon's mines: (1 Kings 7 & 9) were discovered in 1934 by the rabbi and archaeologist Nelson Glueck.
  • The kingdom of Sheba: (1 Kings 10:1) archaeological evidence has shown that it already existed before the time of King Solomon.
  • The Cyrus Cylinder: a stone cylinder (6th century BC) that records decrees for subject peoples to return to their homelands, and decrees offering help to rebuild those peoples' temples. It confirms the statements in the book of Ezra and fulfils Isaiah's prophecy about the Persian king Cyrus.
  • The prism of the Assyrian king Sennacherib: it records the tribute paid by Hezekiah, king of Jerusalem, to the Assyrian king Sennacherib. It also records that Sennacherib lifted the siege of Jerusalem for no stated reason. In 2 Kings 19:35 we read why this happened.
  • Hezekiah's tunnel: a tunnel built in the 7th century BC to secure Jerusalem's water supply in case of a siege by the Assyrian king Sennacherib.

Other finds:

  • The geographical features connected with the Garden of Eden, described in the 2nd chapter of Genesis, have been shown to be real, and their description entirely consistent.
  • In many parts of the Old Testament advice and instructions that really work are given about making bricks, farming, raising livestock, locating unknown areas, and even finding water in desert places
