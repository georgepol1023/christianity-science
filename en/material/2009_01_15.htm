Neo-Darwinism: are mutations a source of new species?

According to the synthetic theory of evolution, or neo-Darwinism, the source of evolutionary change is the mutations that can happen in the genetic material (DNA). Then, under the guiding action of natural selection, a new species can gradually be formed. In this article we will examine whether such a hypothesis can stand.

In an earlier article we discussed in detail the genetic material (DNA), a macromolecule found in the nucleus of every cell of an organism, in which a huge amount of information that determines the structure of each organism, its outward features and the way all its organs and biological systems work is arranged in a wonderful way. All this information is recorded in a “chemical alphabet” that uses 4 “chemical letters”, which are in fact 4 nitrogenous chemical bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G).

But the genetic material can undergo changes in various ways. Changes in the sequence of the “chemical letters” are called mutations, and they usually produce a different phenotype (that is, altered outward features of the individual), although this does not happen with every mutation. Whether or not a different phenotype results depends on how the change affects the way proteins are made.

For example, a change in a single “chemical letter” can lead to the protein haemoglobin being made wrongly, so that the red blood cells are sickle-shaped (sickle-cell anaemia), which causes very serious problems with blood circulation and the working of the organs. Likewise, mutations in genes responsible for making melanin lead to albinism, a condition in which the person has white skin and hair and suffers from various related conditions. But there are also cases where a mutation may have little or no effect on how a protein is made and works, and the person shows no disorder at all (neutral or silent mutations).

Mutations can happen in any cell of the organism. But only mutations in the reproductive cells (egg, sperm) are passed on to offspring, and this is how hereditary diseases arise.

It is very important to stress that the overwhelming majority of mutations are harmful, because they have serious effects on the organism. Indeed, many hereditary diseases and cancers are caused by mutations.

Mutations can happen either in what is called the “spontaneous” way, that is, they appear suddenly because of errors in copying DNA or in the division of chromosomes, or they are caused by environmental factors such as chemicals, X-rays, gamma rays, cosmic radiation, ultraviolet radiation and so on.

Of course, there are special enzymes that repair the errors that happen while DNA is being copied, so that the chance of an error is limited to 1 in 10 trillion (the human genome consists of about 6 billion “chemical letters”). Even so, mutations do happen in general, because of the factors we saw above, and, as we said, the overwhelming majority of them are harmful (some less, some more) or, at best, simply cause no problem (neutral or silent mutations).

Now according to the synthetic theory of evolution, or “neo-Darwinism”, the various kinds of plant and animal organisms that exist today arose from simpler ancestral forms through the gradual change of their characteristics. This change is due to genetic information being added to the DNA of the ancestral forms – an addition that supposedly happened through successive modifications of the genetic material, through a great number of successive favourable mutations that happened entirely by chance. As all these mutations accumulated, and with the help of natural selection, organisms gradually acquired new properties and slowly evolved into higher species.

---------------------------------------------------------------------------------
But such a theory cannot stand, for the following main reasons:
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------- ◊ The problem of probabilities: Mutations are very rare, about 1 in every 10,000,000 (107) duplications of a DNA molecule. Of course, someone might say that this rarity is not a problem when there are many cells (as, for example, in our body, which is made up of about 100 trillion (1014) cells, or even more so in bacterial cultures, where there are many millions of bacteria). But the problem lies in the fact that for an organism to change to such a degree that a new species results, a very large number of mutations is required, which on the one hand must not be harmful, and on the other must each “continue” what the one before it began. Assuming that only non-harmful mutations happen in an organism (an entirely utopian possibility), the probability of just two mutations happening, the one continuing the work of the one before, is 1 in 1014 (100 trillion) and their result would be, for example, merely crinkled wings on a fly. The probability of just four such mutations happening is 1 in 1028 (ten thousand trillion trillion). For such a probability to be realised requires a number of cells that would not fit on our entire planet. Moreover, just four mutations are a long way from changing any organism to such a degree that a new speciesresults. Besides, our Sun is estimated to be about 5 billion years old (1017 seconds) and the universe to contain 1080 atoms. So the whole universe is not big enough, nor our solar system old enough, for probabilities of this order ever to be fulfilled! (for example, the well-known evolutionist Julian Huxley calculated the probability of the horse arising by evolution at 1 in 10300.000!!!).
◊ There are no purely favourable mutations: To this day no mutation has been found that is purely beneficial. There are only isolated cases of mutations that do give an organism a survival advantage , but this advantage holds only under very particular environmental conditions, since it comes at the expense of some other normal function that has been destroyed by the mutation.

For example, carriers of the mutation that causes sickle-cell anaemia are not infected by malaria. But their children have a 25% chance of being born with sickle-cell anaemia and a 25% chance of being as liable as anyone to catch malaria.

Similarly, people who carry a mutation in a gene called CCR5 cannot be infected by the AIDS virus. But this mutation is useful only if they are exposed to the AIDS virus, because in general it brings other serious complications to the body. Specifically, it has been found that people who carry this mutation are more likely to be infected by West Nile virus and hepatitis C. So those who have the mutation have a survival advantage when exposed to the AIDS virus, but those who do not have it have a survival advantage when exposed to West Nile virus or hepatitis C.

So an advantage that a mutation may give is not absolute but relative, since depending on the conditions of the environment it may also be a disadvantage.

Note:
Often the development of resistance to antibiotics in some bacteriais wrongly given as an example of a favourable (beneficial) mutation. First of all, antibiotics work because these drugs are specialised to strike a particular target (a part or a biological function of the bacterium), so that the bacterium is disabled or killed. Resistance to antibiotics can develop in two ways:
  • Either a bacterium undergoes a mutation that modifies one of its parts or one of its biological functions in such a way that the antibiotic does not recognise the target it should “hit”, and so the bacterium is not affected by the antibiotic. But the cost of this “advantage” is a “crippled” bacterium, since such mutations destroy some growth factor (proteins that regulate the multiplication and lifespan of the bacterium), causing serious abnormalities in its biological functions.
  • Or because the bacterium's DNA contains information for resistance to antibiotics. But this information is not the result of evolution (that is, of favourable mutations) that happened because the bacteria were exposed to antibiotics; on the contrary, it has been found that in bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics, the information for developing resistance already existed in their genetic material. A typical example is some of the bacteria revived from the frozen bodies of the explorers of the Franklin expedition to the Arctic (1845), which showed resistance to antibiotics even though antibiotics had not yet been invented!

◊ Mutations lead to degradation: As we said earlier, the overwhelming majority of mutations are harmful. This means that they do not create any new characteristic but alter an existing one. So in a hypothetical scenario of evolution through the accumulation of mutations, the rarity of favourable mutations (if we assume there are any) would have as its overall result the degradation of the organism's functions (if the result were viable), and in no case its improvement towards a higher new species. Besides, the very way in which mutations appear and act, that is, randomness and the tendency towards disorganisation, rules out the creation through mutations of a new characteristic (for example a new limb), because that would require the development of harmonious and organised cooperation between separate parts (nerves, muscles and so on), so that the new limb would not be something superfluous but could carry out a specialised function. But randomness and alteration never bring improvement and organisation, only degradation and disorganisation. This is also what the 2nd law of thermodynamics expresses.
◊ Mutations presuppose a Creator: What mutations act on is the organised genetic information in DNA. So for mutations to happen that will cause evolution, the genetic material and the information must already exist. For mutations cannot create information, only modify information that already exists· cannot add information, only cause information to be lost. This happens because mutations act like typing errors: for there to be typing errors in a text, the text must obviously exist first (and be correct in syntax and grammar, and contain specific information). It is also obvious that typing errors cannot compose a text, only modify one that already exists; so they cannot add information, only cause parts of it to be lost and meanings to be confused, because words or punctuation marks have been altered. So, before we even consider mutations as a mechanism of evolution, we are called to answer the fundamental question of who created the DNA on which they act. For such a molecule cannot be created by purely mechanical processes, as we saw in an earlier article.


◊ Mutations lead to a loss of information: Often macroevolution (that is, evolution that leads to the creation of new species) is wrongly confused with microevolution , that is, the adaptation of an organism to the environmental conditions of the time without its kind changing. The first is an unproven hypothesis, while the second is a real phenomenon which, however, is not due to information added to DNA through mutations, but to information that already existed , which is expressed according to the environmental conditions. One example is skin colour, for which there are genes that can produce various varieties (white, dark, red and so on). But these varieties did not appear through evolution but already existed in the DNA of the first human beings, as we will see in a later article.

So the individuals with gene variants that make them better adapted to their environment prevail, while the individuals carrying the other gene variants gradually disappear over the following generations. For example, a white European couple cannot have children with dark skin, because that population has lost the genetic information for dark skin colour: in those environmental conditions of less sunshine, dark skin is a biological disadvantage, and so the individuals carrying the relevant genes (and so the genes themselves) have, over the generations, disappeared from that population's gene “pool”.

It is worth noting here that this ability to adapt to different environmental conditions also confirms the rightness of God's command: “Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth” (Genesis 1:22 & 28) since people and animals are able to inhabit the Earth over a very wide geographical range, adapting to different environmental conditions by drawing on the right information that has been in their DNA from the beginning.

So we see that the mechanism of natural selection works on the one hand by using genetic information that already exists, and on the other by losing information through adaptation to more specialised conditions. That is, exactly the opposite happens to the view that evolution proceeds by adding information to the genetic material through mutations. This is also why, as they say, “evolution does not go backwards”: if a species is exposed to the conditions in which its ancestors lived, it may well (depending on how different the conditions are) be unable to readapt to them. This happens because for many generations it has lived adapted to a different environment, so the information in its DNA needed to survive in the ancestral conditions has been lost; a typical example is the fruit fly Drosophila (see the related article in the right-hand column: “Evolution does not go backwards”).

From all the above, then, it is clear that mutations cannot be a mechanism for creating new species, or for evolution in general. Indeed, no scientist recommends regular exposure to medical radiation equipment, nor argues that the depletion of ozone (that is, an increase in the levels of ultraviolet radiation) could have beneficial effects or improve living organisms.
