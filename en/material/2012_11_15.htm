Teenagers at risk: how the internet, video games and substances affect brain development

In past decades scientists, having observed an “explosion” of human brain development during pregnancy and up to about the first 18 months of life, believed that brain development is completed by the age of 6–7. But in the past decade, thanks to the development of MRI, a second “wave” of dramatic development, between the ages of 10 and 13, was also observed.

Straight after that, as we saw in an earlier article, from the age of 14 to about 24 the brain begins a process of specialisation (fine-tuning) in which:

  • on the one hand, the nerve synapses for the knowledge and skills a person needs to cope with the conditions of the particular environment they live in are consolidated;
  • on the other, the “unnecessary” nerve synapses are removed (a decrease in grey matter) through a process called “pruning” (“pruning”).

So this is a particularly critical period in which any outside influences, whether positive or negative, decisively affect the development of the brain and may have an impact on the whole of a person's later life.

As for the process of “pruning”, scientists' basic hypothesis is the principle “use it or lose it” (“use it or lose it”). That is, if a teenager is busy, for example, learning a musical instrument, playing a sport, studying for school and so on, their brain is “wired” (shapes its nerve cells and connections) accordingly. If, on the other hand, they are in the habit of idling or spend their time on video games, watching television, music videos and so on, then the corresponding cells and connections will be the ones that survive in their brain.

The above is confirmed by recent research:


On using the internet:
In a study by the University of California (UCLA) in 2008[6], using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), it was observed that the brain of experienced internet users, and mainly the part of the prefrontal lobe, was different from that of people who do not use the internet. The brains of people who were not internet users but began to use it for just one week were also studied. What was striking was the finding that in such a short time the brains of these (novice) users had already begun to be “rewired” (“rewiring”) and to resemble the picture of the brains of experienced users.

A related Chinese study[6] reports that the brain of people addicted to the internet shows a picture similar to the brain of people dependent on drugs and alcohol. Specifically, abnormal white matter and a decrease in grey matter were observed, which has decisive effects on the working of centres for speech, memory, movement, emotions, the senses and so on. Indeed, the more time spent “online”, the greater the signs of brain “atrophy”.


On video games:
A recent report in the Daily Telegraph[7] says that an average child between the ages of 10 and 11 will spend about 2,000 hours in front of a computer screen, which can cause physical changes in the brain, leading to attention and behaviour disorders. Especially when it comes to videogames, which, as is well known, strongly stimulate the senses, scientists say that this “blows the mind”, switching off certain nerve connections in the brain temporarily or permanently.

A study carried out by a collaboration of several universities[8] showed the effect of video games on particular areas of the brain. Some of them are:

  • Under-activity in parts of the frontal cortex, which causes mood swings and the development of aggressive behaviour.
  • Release of dopamine, leading to the development of addiction.
  • Under-activity in areas of the brain that handle conflicting emotions. So when, for example, the player shoots, the negative feeling is spontaneously suppressed so that the game can go on.
  • Players of highly aggressive video games are significantly more anxious than other people.
  • Violent games lead in the short or long term to the development of aggressive feelings, behaviour and thoughts.

Also, as regards games of violence and aggression, research[10] reports that they lead to desensitising the brain to violence and death, while at the same time “programming” it to react aggressively, since through the game it is led to learn that such behaviour is rewarded (extra points, unlocking levels and so on). Then these reactions, as the brain adapts to working this way, show themselves in real life too. The development of the games' graphics, which have reached a very high level of imitating reality, also contributes to this. Indeed, experts describe war video games no longer as “games” but as “combat simulators”, while at the same time young criminals admit that they were inspired and even trained by video games of this kind.

On drug use:
Research by the US National Academy of Sciences[11], overturns the myth that marijuana is a benign drug that is not addictive and does not affect the health of the brain. On the contrary, according to studies of teenagers before and after they started using it, it is linked to a fall in IQ as well as with damage to the brain's neurons, which affects cognitive functions as well as behaviour. Scientists also point out that in adolescence, which is also the period of “pruning” and “rewiring” of the brain, drug use is extremely dangerous since the harmful consequences for the brain may not be able to be put right even if use stops, and so will stay with a person for the rest of their life.

Likewise, prolonged alcohol use can cause cognitive damage of varying degrees to the brain. Indeed, research[13] has shown that the teenage brain is more vulnerable to alcohol than an adult's. Although the brain is able to begin “rewiring” itself properly if alcohol use stops, even in this case there is a chance of permanent damage, mainly to cognitive functions.

In connection with all of the above, many scientists raise the question of how guidance can be given by parents, teachers and older people in general for making the right use of the potential of the teenage brain. Bridging neuroscience with everyday life and the practical application of scientific conclusions is a great challenge.

The neuroscientist Jay Giedd of the US National Institute of Mental Health comments that the more we see science advance, the more often it gives us directions pointing backwards, leading us to old, classic family principles and habits. He says that the best scientific advice is what our grandmother gave for generation after generation: to spend quality time with our children in an atmosphere of love.

Indeed, research[4] shows that because the human brain has an extensive network of nerve connections for developing social relationships (especially with those who care for it), teenagers want to spend more time and have more communication with their parents, even when they seem to push them away. At such a decisive time, the worst response of parents would be to “get rid of” their child by giving them video games, the internet, DVDs and so on.

Here once again the value of Holy Scripture is shown, which is the Word of God, since it commends to us all these family values which in our day have been forgotten or devalued, so that family relationships have become a major problem of our time.

To close the article, we would like to say that the most decisive “rewiring” of the brain, which can happen at any age and which transforms a person's way of thinking from the depths, is new birth, that is, the spiritual birth that makes a person a child of God, set free from corrupting desires and from bondage to passions such as those mentioned above. Within the person there takes place a continuing renewal of the mind, which leads them to become like Jesus Christ:

“And be renewed in the spirit of your mind; and that ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness” (Ephesians 4:23–24)

And new birth comes about through the Word of God and through the Holy Spirit (John 3:3) when a person accepts the sacrifice of Jesus Christ on the Cross for their sins, and believes in His resurrection.
