The problem of unemployment and how to face it in Christ

A major issue of our time, which touches all of us directly or indirectly and whose extent is causing cracks in the fabric of society, is the issue of unemployment, which, among other things, has a decisive effect on a person's state of mind.

The purpose of work is not only to secure the means needed to survive; it also gives a person self-respect and dignity, and in general shapes their social identity. So, according to recent research, unemployment, besides being a problem of meeting the needs of life, also turns out to be a problem of human psychology. The main points of the psychological side of the problem are a sense of losing one's social identity, a lack of motivation to be occupied and an upset of balance, whether in personal relationships or in managing one's (now) free time (that is, a lack of motivation to use it creatively, so that time is wasted in idleness and one's state of mind gets worse).

The size of the unemployment problem can be felt from recent statistics (August 2012):

  • More than 25 million unemployed in the countries of the European Union.
  • A record unemployment rate in the eurozone: 11.4% (that is, more than 18 million unemployed).
  • The countries with the highest unemployment rates are Spain (25.1%) and Greece (24.4%). In these countries more than 50% of people under 25 are unemployed.
  • Apart from the countries regarded as Europe's “periphery”, the situation is bad in the “core” too, with the French unemployment rate at 10.6% (that is, more than 3 million unemployed).
  • Unemployment is one of the main causes of the unprecedented rise in suicidal tendencies in Greece, by more than 20% in the last 3 years.

Looking at the issue through the lens of Holy Scripture, which is the Word of God, it should first be noted that the first to establish work was God Himself, when He gave the first man He formed, Adam, the task of dressing and keeping the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2:15). So being occupied in work is a normal and healthy condition for man, with the difference that, unlike today's situation, God's original purpose was not for work to be a painful process that would burden His creature, but an occupation that would give him pleasure, coming from using the abilities with which he is gifted and from then enjoying the fruits of his labour:

“And also that every man should eat and drink, and enjoy the good of all his labour, it is the gift of God” (Ecclesiastes 3:13)

Even the great spiritual figures of Holy Scripture were not idle but were occupied in some work to meet the needs of life. For example, the apostle Peter was a fisherman (Matthew 4:18), while the apostle Paul was a tentmaker (Acts 18:3), a trade he often practised during his apostolic journeys so as not to be a burden to his Christian brothers and sisters (Acts 20:33–35), and also to help the poor (Galatians 2:10).

But can a person of God face unemployment and financial hardship in their life? The answer is yes, since there are a great many such cases in the Word of God:
  • Abraham, while he was waiting for God's promise to be fulfilled, faced famine in the land where he was living and moved to Egypt (Genesis 12).
  • Jacob and his 11 sons, because of famine, looked for provisions in Egypt, where they were reunited with their lost brother Joseph and were kept alive, so that from them the people of Israel would be formed, through whom Christ would come into the world (Genesis 45:7).
  • When the prophet Elijah was being persecuted and hiding in the wilderness and was unable to work, God fed him in an unexpected way: the ravens brought him bread and meat, and he drank water from a brook. When the brook dried up because there was no rain, God led Elijah to a widow in Sidon, who fed him from a barrel of meal that did not run out and a cruse of oil that did not fail (1 Kings 17).
  • The prophet's widow in debt, who turned to the prophet Elisha, and the Lord worked the miracle of multiplying the oil (2 Kings 4:1–7).

In all these cases God's promise was fulfilled, that “all things work together for good to them that love God” (Romans 8:28). So when such situations come into our lives, they are not God's punishment for our sins (Christ was punished for those in our place), nor a sign that God has abandoned us. And how could He abandon us, since for the sake of each one of us, while we did not yet know Him, He became man and died on the cross?

Always behind events and apparent difficulties, God, who also cares about our work, is working something for our spiritual good, above all shifting our trust from earthly supports to His person. People who have entrusted themselves to Christ should know that whatever situation they find themselves in, God is in complete control of the circumstances and will not let them be lost or die of hunger:

“But the very hairs of your head are all numbered. Fear ye not therefore” (Matthew 10:30–31) “Therefore take no thought*, saying, What shall we eat? or, What shall we drink? or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed? (For after all these things do the Gentiles seek:) for your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things. But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you” (Matthew 6:31–33) (*not in the sense of neglecting our everyday affairs, but in the sense of not being anxious.)

That is exactly why facing a problem such as unemployment starts from a person's personal relationship with Christ and working together with Him, doing the things that are ours to do:

  • Prayer and study of the Word of God at home and in church, together with striving to align ourselves with the will of God.
  • Efforts to look for work with a humble attitude, accepting that we may have to adapt to working environments not worthy of our qualifications, or even settle for lower pay.
  • Our actions should not be such that we “run ahead” of the Lord with hasty initiatives (because we will end up in worse dead ends), but we should humbly ask for His guidance so that we follow Him where we see He is leading us. And for this kind of sight and understanding we need – especially in such cases – to persevere in prayer, in the study of the Word of God and in obedience to His will, as we said above.

In the book of Ruth there is a typical example of the above, where a man named Elimelech, because of a famine in Israel, decided to leave God's land with his wife and his two sons and live among the nations in the land of Moab. But going out from the presence of God, without divine guidance and relying on his own actions, the result was that soon he himself and his two sons died, and in the end his wife returned to Israel, worn out, with one of her daughters-in-law, Ruth.

So calling on the Lord and waiting for His guidance is our safety in times of unemployment and hardship.

So we will need to go through a period of hardship, since it is written that “we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God” (Acts 14:22).

Besides, it is a fact that through difficulties we come to know more of the Lord's character and His intentions for us, and so our faith and love for Him grow. The Lord also seeks to draw our attention away from attachment to earthly things and focus it on the true treasure and on eternal values.

However great the trial, it has an end date. And all these things are temporary, and do not compare with what the Lord has prepared for us to enjoy for ever if we endure: “For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us” (Romans 8:18) “But he that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved” (Matthew 24:13).
