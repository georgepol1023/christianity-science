DNA: how genetic information is organised and preserved

Inside the nucleus of every cell of every living organism is the DNA molecule, in which all the information about the structure and functions of the cell is stored, and so all the information about the features of an organism's structure and the working of all its organs and systems.

DNA belongs to the class of nucleic acids and is an extremely complex and large molecule (a macromolecule). For example, in the nucleus of each of the roughly 100 trillion cells of a human being there are about 2 metres of DNA! But thanks to its astonishing ability to fold, it manages to fit into a space of about 10 millionths of a metre!

The structure of the DNA molecule is like a spiral metal staircase (with a right-handed twist). This form was presented in 1953 by Watson and Crick and was named the “double helix model”. The sides of this staircase (the “handrails”) are made of chemical compounds called polysaccharides, while each “step” is made of two nitrogenous bases. These are chemical compounds that occur in DNA in 4 different forms: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C), and they are the 4 chemical letters in which all the information stored in DNA is recorded. The two nitrogenous bases that make up each step are joined to each other according to the rule of complementarity: an adenine joins only with a thymine, and a guanine only with a cytosine. So each step is made of either an A–T pair or a G–C pair.

Each of the two “handrails”, together with the “half” steps that belong to it (each half step is one chemical letter), forms a polynucleotide chain. So the DNA molecule is made up of two polynucleotide chains. If we run along one polynucleotide chain, reading its chemical letters and dividing them into threes, then each sequence of three chemical letters forms a chemical word, just as a particular sequence of letters of the alphabet on a sheet of paper corresponds to a particular word of our language. Each chemical word gives the information for making a particular amino acid.

So a particular sequence of chemical letters forms a particular sequence of triplets – chemical words – which in turn give the information for making a particular sequence of amino acids, so that a particular protein is formed. This particular sequence of letters that results in a particular protein is called a gene. So DNA contains genes, each of which has, as we saw, chemical words for producing a protein, and also chemical punctuation marks, whose role is to separate the genes from one another, just as the words of a text are divided into sentences with the help of punctuation marks.

But besides the genes, the DNA molecule also contains long stretches with no information for producing proteins.

UPDATE:
In September 2012 the “ENCODE” research project presented an “updated edition” of the “encyclopedia of DNA”, which revealed the discovery that the part of DNA until then regarded as “junk” is a huge hidden “operating system” that controls the human genome. In all, about 80% of DNA has now been shown to carry out some kind of useful biochemical function (for more details see source no. 10 in the right-hand column).

The process by which the information for producing a protein is retrieved from the DNA molecule is called transcription, while the process of reading this information and then building the protein is called translation. Both processes are carried out by a series of extremely complex cellular machines, which literally form a production line of astonishing speed and precision. Indeed, many geneticists compare the cell to a car factory equipped with a great number of intricate machines that nevertheless work in a coordinated way, making parts that fit together and form the structural and functional elements of the cell, and so of the organism. Another kind of nucleic acid, RNA, plays a decisive role in these processes.

The DNA molecule also has the ability to make faithful copies of itself with astonishing speed and precision, so that the genetic information is passed on unchanged to new cells and so to an organism's offspring. The precision with which the cellular machinery copies DNA, together with mechanisms for checking and repair, prevents it from being altered, reducing the chance of a copying error to 1 in 10 billion! This means that all these processes favour and safeguard the stability of the genetic material, not its variability, which in the long run would alter the characteristics of the species towards the formation of a new one, as the theory of evolution holds. In conclusion, the mechanisms that pass genetic information from generation to generation are built to prevent any alteration – in other words, to resist the “evolution” of the species!

From 1961 onwards the genetic code began to be deciphered, that is, which amino acid corresponds to each of the chemical words in DNA. We could compare this to deciphering an unknown script carved on an ancient stone tablet. On the basis of this comparison, let us focus on some important features of the DNA molecule:


The DNA molecule contains information
Although at first we do not know the meaning of the carvings of the inscription, we can still see that there is some information written on this stone. This at once rules out the possibility that these shapes were carved on the stone by random natural processes. For nature can never produce information. Random natural processes can produce irregular shapes or, at best, repeating patterns (for example the even ripples made on sand by the waves, or the crystal lattices of salts formed by electrostatic forces). But producing information requires the intervention of an intelligent being. For the information in DNA to have been recorded by random natural processes would require the waves and the wind to form on the sand not a few ripples, but the entire Encyclopaedia Britannica!
The DNA molecule has specified complexity
The reason we realise that the carvings on the stone are a kind of writing, and so contain information, is that they have specified complexity. This means, first of all, that this is not a constantly repeating pattern, because that would be something specified but not complex. On the other hand, the carvings on the stone are complex, but their complexity is not entirely random (chaotic) but specified. Indeed, the experts' studies will find that there is a logic in the way the shapes have been carved (rules of grammar and syntax), and it is on the basis of this logic that the unknown script will be deciphered.

Likewise, the DNA molecule is extremely complex, but with a complexity that is not chaotic but specified, because it obeys rules of such logic that scientists regard its contents as equivalent to a “code” and a “script”. Random natural processes can produce either something specified but not complex, or something complex but not specified. To build something with specified complexity, the intervention of an intelligent creator is required. All the more so when it is, according to specialist geneticists, the most efficient means of storing information in the universe! (a teaspoon of DNA could hold the information of all the genes of all organisms, with room to spare for the information in all the books ever written!)

The above overturns the hypothesis of the evolution of species from another angle too. The genetic material of a single-celled organism (for example a bacterium) contains about 600 genes, and as we move up to higher kinds of living organisms the number of genes increases step by step (30–40 thousand genes in man). If evolution really took place, producing the more complex organisms from the simplest ones, how could a huge amount of organised genetic information have been added to the small original amount of DNA, entirely by chance, and within an extremely short period of time? (see the “Cambrian explosion”, which we will discuss in a later article). In other words, how did something with specified complexity develop without the intervention of intelligence?

Most often, people try to answer these questions by referring to the working of the laws of physics and chemistry and to the chemical properties of the individual parts that make up DNA. But that is like trying to explain why a sentence printed on paper makes sense by referring to the physical and chemical properties of the ink. Those properties explain only why the ink stays on the paper, and not why the ink is arranged on the paper in such a way as to carry information.

The final blow to the theory that DNA could be a product of chance comes from newer findings in chemistry, since producing the component parts of nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), even in the laboratory, is extremely difficult and would be impossible without special handling and strictly controlled conditions, which makes the chance formation of nucleic acids in the far more hostile primordial soup impossible.

Besides, proteins are needed to make DNA and for all the functions it performs. But to make proteins, instructions from DNA are needed. So at the start of life, which formed first: the proteins or the nucleic acids? This brings us to the well-known riddle: “which came first, the chicken or the egg?” For the one cannot exist without the other. All known forms of life have both proteins and nucleic acids at the same time. But neither of the two, as we have said, could have been formed first by random chemical processes. And the alternative scenario of an “RNA world” as the cradle of life (see the related material) faces insurmountable problems from the newer findings.

So it is the duty of the scientific community not to misinterpret the data in an effort to defend prejudices dressed in a cloak of scientific appearance, but to present the natural conclusion that follows from the data, namely that the phenomenon of life bears witness to the existence of a creative Mind.
