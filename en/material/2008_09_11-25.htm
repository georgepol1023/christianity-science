The LHC accelerator experiment at CERN

Modern physics is focused on the study of what are called elementary particles, the particles that are the building blocks of matter. Although these particles are extremely small, the experimental equipment needed to study them keeps getting bigger. This is because handling particles so small, in order to control them so that they show their properties, requires very specialised facilities and very large experimental machines, called accelerators.

Accelerators are experimental machines that can accelerate thin beams of elementary particles to very high speeds – so high that they approach the speed of light. The particle beams are then deflected from their path so that they collide with one another at very high energy, because of the speed they have gained. The collisions can reveal the properties of these particles, and other particles are also formed, from whose presence the researchers can draw very important conclusions.

The most modern accelerator is the LHC (Large Hadron Collider), at the CERN centre for nuclear research on the French–Swiss border. The LHC is essentially a circular underground tunnel (a ring) at an average depth of 100 metres, with a circumference of 27 kilometres. Its purpose is to accelerate 2 beams of protons to speeds almost equal to the speed of light. When the beams collide, it is believed that the conditions that existed a few moments after the so-called Big Bang will be recreated (see the topic In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth), which will allow the study of how matter came together to form the universe as we know it today. The protons are accelerated to such speeds with special, very large superconducting magnets, which for this purpose must operate at a temperature of -271oC. The beams will not be given their maximum speed (and so the maximum energy for the collisions) from the start, but step by step. When this maximum energy is reached (7 TeV for each beam, 14 TeV in total), the two proton beams will be equivalent to two high-speed trains weighing 400 tonnes each, colliding while each travels at 150 kilometres an hour. Locally and for an instant, temperatures 100,000 times higher than those at the centre of the Sun (13.6 million degrees Celsius) will be reached.

The results of the collisions are collected mainly at four points around the LHC, where four large detectors (the size of a block of flats) are installed for this purpose: ATLAS, CMS, ALICE and LHCb.

This is an extremely complex undertaking employing about 10,000 scientists from 500 universities around the world. Greece's participation is also notable; it is one of the founding members of CERN, since 1954. Professors and students from Greece have been sent to work on the control software, the data analysis software, the construction and testing of parts of the ATLAS detector, and other parts of the experiment.

In these detectors, installed at particular points around the LHC, the energy, path and other parameters of the particles produced in the collisions are recorded as the collisions take place. This data is recorded, and its volume is so great that, even after “filtering” out the useful information, over one year it amounts to a volume of data 10,000 times greater than the information in all the books printed in the whole world in a year.

So it is clear that the data from the experiment cannot be transferred, stored and processed locally at CERN, however well equipped it is. For this reason CERN, together with 33 countries, created a special network structure to achieve shared computing power and data storage: the LHC Computing GRID. The GRID covers almost the whole planet, is based entirely on fibre-optic lines for faster data transmission, and at present includes about 100,000 computers.

In brief, the main aims of the LHC detectors are:

  • To test whether the Higgs particle exists or not. This is a particle thought to be responsible for the process by which any particle that forms acquires mass.

    UPDATE:
     In July 2012 the scientists at CERN announced that in the experiments carried out around the LHC accelerator they had detected a particle which, with a fairly high level of certainty (it has not yet been fully confirmed), they can say must be the Higgs boson (see articles no. 27 & 28 in the related material in the right-hand column).
  • To complete or revise the Standard Model, the model that describes particle physics as we know it today.
  • To try to unify the four fundamental forces of nature into one (gravity, electromagnetism, the weak & the strong nuclear force)
  • To recreate the conditions a few moments after the Big Bang so as to study how the universe has been forming since then.
  • To study supersymmetry and extra dimensions (see further explanations in the related material in the right-hand column).

There are also implications for everyday life, connected with the effort to discover and use new properties of matter, while the needs of the experiment also drove the development of materials, computing and network technology. The study of radiation from elementary particles is also very useful in developing medical imaging and treatment methods.

Through all this effort and interest in studying elementary particles, we see that scientists are seeking to investigate the events of the beginning of the life of the universe and its development since then. In this way the Word of God is confirmed, because while for many centuries scientists believed that the universe was infinite and eternal, Holy Scripture declared that there is a “beginning” and that the universe is expanding and is therefore finite – findings that scientists made only at the beginning of the 20th century (see the topic In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth).

Moreover, through experiments studying elementary particles, scientists find that the behaviour of the building blocks of matter is governed by laws and equations, and by a wonderful harmony that they are trying to analyse. They observe that the mathematical equations describing the principles of physics are not irregularly complicated but are marked by simplicity and symmetry. This is also why the universe we live in is not chaotic but obeys specific laws. And the conclusion that follows logically and naturally is that since there are laws, there must also be a Lawgiver, a Mind that created all these and set them working.

Holy Scripture reveals this person to us:

“For by him (Christ) were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him: and he is before all things, and by him all things consist” (Colossians 1:16–17)
