The history, critique and consequences of evolutionary thought

The theory of the evolution of species, first set out in an organised form in 1859 by Charles Darwin, deeply influenced the way many people think, and so played a decisive part in the course of tragic historical events of the 20th century.

The theory of evolution is not an idea first put forward by Darwin. In fact Darwin built on ideas and views that already existed about the creation and origin of the different species of living organisms – many of them belonging purely to the realm of imagination – and dressed them in a cloak of scientific appearance.

Here are some of the evolutionary ideas that came before him:

  • Anaximander (6th century BC): he believed that life was created by the effect of the Sun's rays on the mud of the sea bed. In this way fish-like animals developed, from which man also descends.
  • Empedocles (5th century BC): he believed that at some point certain plants began to degenerate and, instead of growing leaves, grew human limbs. So at first monsters were created, and then through processes of adaptation we arrived at man.
  • Leucippus & Democritus (5th – 4th century BC): they believed that all beings, even the gods, came from atoms that began to join together.

Representatives of the opposite view were Plato and Aristotle (4th century BC), who did not accept chance and evolution but believed in the existence of a Creator, and that the kinds of living things were fixed and unchanging. Their views prevailed until the 17th century.

In the 17th and 18th centuries ideas about the evolution of species through adaptability, the influence of the environment and heredity began to be put forward again, chiefly by Fr. Bacon, Leibniz, Kant, Buffon and Carl Linnaeus.

Fertile ground for these ideas was provided by the ideological and social setting of Europe in the 16th and 17th centuries, when people began to react against the spiritual darkness and oppression of the Middle Ages, with which, sadly, the name of God and Holy Scripture had become associated. At the same time, the exploration and discovery of new lands brought new species of animals and plants, and also human races, to public attention, and science was called on to explain their origin.

Evolutionary ideas began to spread more widely and to gain an appearance of science, mainly through the work of:

  • Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744 – 1829): he believed in the principle of the use and disuse of bodily organs (those that are used grow and develop, while those not used weaken and disappear), and in the inheritance of acquired characteristics.
  • Erasmus Darwin (1731 – 1802): Charles Darwin's grandfather. He believed that the basic law of nature is “eat or be eaten”, through which the strong and gifted will survive and improve from generation to generation, while the weak are regarded as unnecessary and must be wiped out.

As the culmination of all this, in 1859 Charles Darwin published his work “On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection”.

Earlier, in 1831, at the age of 22, he had joined the voyage of the English ship “Beagle”, whose aim was to chart the coasts of South America. During this five-year voyage Darwin observed the peculiarities of South American nature and was struck by the geographical distribution of species. He noticed that various species of animals, though similar to one another, showed clear adaptations to their particular environment. In Darwin's mind these adaptive changes were recorded as evidence of evolution from lower species.

By about 1840 Darwin had developed the main points of his theory, according to which the small changes a species shows as it adapts to its environment over “unbelievably long” periods of time, being inherited, caused one species to evolve from another. So, according to Darwin, the enormous variety we see in nature today came about by gradual evolution from a single-celled organism, which arose from the chance joining of organic molecules, which in turn arose from the chance joining of inorganic molecules in a primordial soup!

At the end of the 19th century evolutionary thinking was adopted by well-known figures such as Bertrand Russell, Voltaire, Nietzsche and others, who were utterly opposed to every religious, and above all Christian, sentiment. Nietzsche in particular, since he had a great influence on leaders such as Hitler, Stalin and Mussolini, laid the ideological framework for the mass murders of the 20th century.

The main effects of evolutionary thought can be summed up as follows:

  • On Nazism: Hitler used Nietzsche's philosophy as a motive to eliminate the weak and inferior, so as to “help evolution”. In this climate the eugenics movement was born and racism grew – subjects we will discuss in more detail in a later article.
  • On communism: the theory of evolution was the scientific basis of Marxism. Marx himself believed that since man is merely an animal, he is expendable.
    In Mao Tse-tung's China, Darwin's theory was the basis of Chinese scientific socialism. The communists in China believed in evolution through revolution. Stalin's Russian purge killed millions, while in the Cultural Revolution of the 1960s Mao Tse-tung killed more than 30 million people.
  • The slave trade: races such as the black people of Africa, the Native Americans and the Aboriginal people of Australia were regarded as evolutionarily lower human forms, which fully justified conquering them and using them as slaves.
  • Capitalism: the strongest and most able industrialists, wiping out weaker competitors by any means, were in effect fulfilling the natural law of the survival of the fittest.
  • On social ideology: at the end of the 19th century the philosopher Herbert Spencer went so far as to argue that every practice of supporting the weak, such as state welfare, hospital care and even almsgiving, prevents natural selection from doing its good work.

In the coming articles we will look in detail at the scientific evidence showing that today the theory of evolution has collapsed. But from the start it was clear that it was never a scientific theory.

For a natural phenomenon to be studied scientifically, it must be possible to carry out either observation of it in nature or an experiment in the laboratory. Of course, even before observation or experiment, theories are put forward: hypothetical explanations of the phenomenon, which are either confirmed or refuted by the conclusions drawn from observation or experiment.

So, whether a theory later proves right or wrong, for it to be called “scientific” it must be open to criticism and testing; that is, it must necessarily be possible to subject it to either observation or experiment. If neither observation nor experiment is possible, the theory in question can in no way be called “scientific”.

The appearance of life on Earth and the creation of the plant and animal species are events that can be the subject of neither observation nor experiment. So the theories of evolution and abiogenesis are not scientific theories but the products of unproven hypotheses that come from the realm of imagination.

Evidence and finds (rocks, fossils and so on) may be collected about such past events, but they can in no way serve as criteria to confirm or refute the theory of evolution or of abiogenesis so that we could call them “scientific theories”. So it becomes clear that accepting these theories is purely a matter of subjective belief and not of scientific understanding.
