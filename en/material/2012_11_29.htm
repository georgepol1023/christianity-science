Relationships between parents and teenagers

======================== ◄ Relationships between parents and teenagers ► ========================

The cornerstone for building a healthy relationship between parents and children is the teaching of the Word of God, since in it are contained all the necessary principles of upbringing as well as how to apply them. That is why the exhortation is given: “Only take heed to thyself, and keep thy soul diligently, lest thou forget the things which thine eyes have seen, and lest they depart from thy heart all the days of thy life: but teach them thy sons, and thy sons' sons” (Deuteronomy 4:9)

Recent research by Columbia University in New York, aiming to show how the structure of the family affects children's involvement in drugs, alcohol and violence, showed the following:

  • In a single-parent family where there is only a mother, a child is 30% more likely to become involved in drugs, alcohol and violence than a child who grows up with both parents.
  • In a family where both parents are present but the child's relationship with the father is bad, the child is 68% more likely to become involved in the above (that is, compared with the first finding, it would be better if there were no father than for him to be there but not have a good relationship with the children).
  • In a family where both parents are present and the relationships with the children are good, especially with the father, the above likelihood is less than 6%.

So two decisive factors emerge:

  • The structure of the family (single-parent, or whether both parents are present).
  • The functioning of the family as regards the relationships between its members.
FBI research , aiming to sketch the profile of a young person who potentially could become a murderer who might, for example, carry out a mass killing of classmates, concluded that the main common characteristic of such people is either the physical absence of the father or the father's absence from parenting, even though he may be physically present in the home.

Finally, research by one of the most famous medical schools, at Dartmouth, examined 260 separate scientific studies from other institutions on young people and found that from the moment of birth a human being is biologically “built” to develop personal relationships. In its conclusions this study says that if a parent wants to pass on their values to their children, they must do at least two things:

1st) to develop a close, loving relationship with the child; otherwise the child will soon move away from the values the parent stands for. 2nd) to be an example of the values they proclaim.

So what science is finding today, the Word of God has been saying for more than 2,000 years, through the mouth of Jesus Christ:

“For I have given you an example, that ye should do as I have done to you” (John 13:15)

As for the relationship of love and communication between parent and child, there are some basic principles that can help to cultivate it, applied in the order in which they are listed:

1. Acceptance:
Expressing love in such a way that the child comes to believe that whatever they do, their parent loves them. This leads to the development of a sense of security because the child is assured that they have a value for their parents that is a given and is not determined by their good or bad behaviour, by their success or failure. Of course bad behaviour is not approved and grieves the parents, but it must be made clear that even then the parents' love and acceptance continue. A typical example of unconditional acceptance in Holy Scripture is the story of the prodigal son (see Luke 15:11–32). The father did not approve of his son's behaviour and way of life. But when the son saw how low he had sunk and decided to go back, his father did not reproach him for his behaviour, nor had his love for him grown less; he accepted him and restored him.

In the same way, God's love for man and His acceptance are a given, and do not depend on whether a person is a saint or a sinner. If someone lives in sin, of course, they do not taste God's love (because no relationship has been formed), but it remains a given towards them and waits for them to return:

“But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8). 2. Appreciation:
It adds to acceptance a sense of significance. Appreciation grows through sincere praise and approval of their successes – however small – and achievements of the child. This does not mean setting aside discipline and correction whenever they are needed. On the contrary, the child's sense that the parent notices and approves of whatever is done right creates such a positive climate that there is fertile ground for applying discipline and correction. In Holy Scripture this principle (that is, appreciation) is applied again and again in the epistles of the apostle Paul. While the epistles aim to correct what was wrong in each church and to bring discipline in line with God's will, the apostle Paul takes great care to begin each epistle with praise and approval for the struggles and spiritual victories of the members of each church. 3. Love – affection:
It means words and actions that show care for the child and give them a sense of being lovable. This is a human need at any age and leads to a close bond and relationship of trust between parent and child. Research shows that people who did not taste enough love from their parents in childhood went looking for it in adolescence from other people, either by entering into early sexual relationships before marriage or by getting involved in substance abuse and illegal gangs. Holy Scripture is literally full of verses that expressly state the love of God for man. God Himself is love (see 1 John 4:16). But this is not a love that remains only at the level of words and theory; God proved His love in action, by becoming man Himself in the person of Jesus Christ and sacrificing Himself on the cross for man's sake: “For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). 4. Availability:
It means giving time to the child, and it is the practical proof of the three previous principles; otherwise words alone about acceptance, appreciation and love sound false to the child's ears. Giving time and getting involved in the child's problems and affairs (however trivial they may seem by an adult's measure) gives them the conviction that they matter to their parents , since the time they give them shows how high a priority they have in their lives. Even if the parents' free time is limited because of their obligations, their availability to their children is absolutely essential. Some busy parents say that they may not give a great quantity of time to their children but that this little time is at least quality time. But that is unrealistic , since quality time is not something that can come about “at the push of a button” and “to order” within a few minutes; rather, quality moments emerge and arise within a certain quantity of time.

Although God is not subject to limits of time, He shows His availability in practice to everyone who calls on Him:

“And call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me” (Psalm 50:15). “God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble” (Psalm 46:1) 5. Responsibility:
When the parent is the first to show willingness to take on their responsibilities and to be accountable wherever and whenever necessary (even to their own children), then besides becoming a more mature and balanced person themselves, they also teach their children in the best way to be responsible, to be disciplined and to submit to the duties and obligations that fall to them.

In the same way Jesus Christ, carrying out responsibly the mission His heavenly Father entrusted to Him and submitting in all things to His will, was the first to give us the example of submission and obedience to the will of God:

“Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men: and being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross” (Philippians 2:5–8).

Besides, a parent who shows responsibility inspires trust in the moves they make and the decisions they take for the family. This opens the way for their children to accept counsel and discipline.

A typical example in Holy Scripture is Noah , since although God spoke only to him about the coming Flood, his whole family accepted the seemingly strange instruction to build the ark, because their father was a trustworthy person and they were sure that he really had instructions from God. 6. Discipline:
For the smooth running of all institutions and social relationships, it is essential to set and keep clear limits and rules. In the opposite case, relationships between people become chaotic and nothing can work properly. So the same must apply within the family.

Discipline of this kind is necessary, because it gives children a sense of security about how the family works, and also of personal safety and protection, since it is made clear that things, and the people themselves, are not out of control or at the mercy of their desires.

But it must be stressed here that for it to be applied successfully, such discipline requires that a relationship of love has first been built through the five previous principles.

Once this has been done, then when the parent needs to discipline the child, it may be done strictly, but the child, however much the discipline displeases them for the moment, will be sure within 1st, that it is for their good and 2nd, that their parent's motive is love.

In this case too, the first example is given by God. After all, the family, like the church, is an institution established by God, and although “where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty” (2 Corinthians 3:17), God has established orders of authority, and there are spiritual laws too. In the church there are ministries and governments, while in the family there are the parents, with the father as the head of the family (not in the sense of a tyrant, but in the sense of the one responsible for the family's course).

Also, at a personal level, when God takes a person through trials or disciplines them, it is clear (because of the loving relationship that has been built beforehand) that this is done for the person's good and that God's motive is love (see Hebrews 12:5–11).

Finally, it should be noted that in some cases where counsel does not seem to bear fruit, then perhaps, depending on the case, the parent may need to let the child taste the consequences of their actions, so that they learn at least through them.

In the story of the prodigal son, such a good father cannot have failed to give his children counsel in their childhood years. Yet with his younger son this counsel found no response. That is why the father did not stop him from leaving home, so that he would learn at least from the consequences of his actions.

To close, we would like to raise one more very important factor. For a parent may apply all the above in the best way, following the instructions of the Word of God, but there is always the “human” factor (which includes bad moments, mistakes, weaknesses and faults), which, together with possible unexpected circumstances, may lead to wrong handling and behaviour.

So it is absolutely essential that parents bring their children, and themselves too, before the Lord in prayer, so that He takes charge of these difficult circumstances and also strengthens them through the Holy Spirit to show wisdom and composure at critical moments. And it is good for this prayer to be made, if possible, by the whole family together (the family altar), so that parents and children grow in unity through this shared striving, and the children themselves learn by example from their parents' spiritual habits.

That is why every Christian family must not neglect:

  • the family altar, with all the members of the family taking an active part.
  • regular reading and study together of the Word of God by the whole family.
  • its regular attendance and participation in the local church.
  • the children's participation in Sunday school and in the youth meetings.

Because we know that the good work will be accomplished:
“Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts” (Zechariah 4:6)

and that: “Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it” (Psalm 127:1).
