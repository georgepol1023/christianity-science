The archaeological and historical reliability of the New Testament

The New Testament is the most reliable book of antiquity. Archaeological finds and historical references agree on this and confirm it; their number and weight set the New Testament before our eyes no longer as a theological text, but as a historical text with theological content.

The four Gospels are in full harmony and agreement with one another, so, without overlooking the reliability of all the Gospels, we will look in more detail at the Gospel of Luke.

The evangelist Luke was born in Antioch and lived in the 1st century AD; he was a physician by profession and a co-worker of the apostle Paul. He is the writer of the Gospel that bears his name and of the book of the Acts of the Apostles, which he wrote after thorough study and questioning of the eyewitnesses of the life of Jesus Christ and of the Apostles, and from events at which he was himself present.

In the two books of the evangelist Luke we find references to:

  • Names of Roman emperors
  • Names of Roman governors & officials
  • Names of leading members of the Jewish priesthood
  • Local offices of the time (e.g. politarchs)
  • Historical events with a great impact on the world of the time (persecutions, famines)
  • Accurate medical & nautical knowledge
  • 32 countries, 54 cities and 9 islands

Almost all of the above references have been confirmed precisely by archaeological finds and historical sources. This shows that the evangelist Luke set out to record the events exactly as they happened, with a care that would mark a great historian.

Some archaeological finds for the New Testament:

Ancient Capernaum: discovered in 1838; in 1866 the ruins of the synagogue were identified. The pool of Bethesda: discovered in 1956; it has five porches, as John 5:2 says. The Pilate inscription: in 1961 an inscription in Latin bearing the name of Pontius Pilate was discovered at Caesarea. Lysanias: In a temple in Damascus the only evidence was found for Lysanias, tetrarch of Abilene, who is mentioned in Luke 3:1 The “politarchs”: In 1962 inscriptions were published that mentioned this office, which appears nowhere in ancient Greek literature but only in the Acts of the Apostles 17:6 & 8. The Erastus inscription: Discovered in 1929 in Corinth, it shows that Erastus really was the city's treasurer, as Paul says in Romans 16:23. The theatre at Ephesus: where the rioters gathered (Acts 19:23–41), has also been excavated, and inscriptions have been found that stress the city's worship of the goddess Artemis.

Some historical evidence from outside the Bible:

Flavius Josephus: A Jewish historian whose works include an important reference to the person of Christ, known as the “Testimonium Flavianum”. Flavius Josephus also gives interesting accounts of other officials of his time (1st century AD). Tacitus: A Roman historian who refers to Christ as a historical person and the founder of Christianity. Suetonius: A Roman historian who records the expulsion of the Jews from Rome, an event that matches the account in Acts 18:1–2. The Talmud: The body of Pharisaic traditions meant to interpret and supplement the Law. It contains a historical reference to Jesus of Nazareth as a great transgressor of the Law. H.G. Wells (1866 – 1946): a famous English writer who wrote “The Outline of History”. He was an atheist, but he used the Gospels as historical sources for his work and held that the story of Christ cannot be a human invention. W. Durant (1885 – 1981): professor of the philosophy of history at Columbia University. His best-known work is the 11-volume “The Story of Civilization”. Its 3rd volume is entitled “Caesar and Christ” and deals at length with the person of Christ. He did not believe that the New Testament was given by inspiration of God. In this part of his work, among other things, he turns against those who believe that Jesus Christ was a myth and never existed.

The Messianic prophecies:

There are more than 300 prophecies in the Old Testament that foretell the coming of the Messiah. In the New Testament we see that all these prophecies are fulfilled in one person, Jesus Christ. It is not very hard, even for an ordinary student, to make a few calculations to estimate the probability that each of these prophecies would be fulfilled (for more details, please see the related material in the right-hand column). Even so, formal studies by a Christian college, for just 20 Messianic prophecies, give a probability of their being fulfilled in a single person of 1 in 10175!

In conclusion, to question the reliability of Holy Scripture is unreasonable, since archaeology, the study of historical documents and, as we finally saw, even mathematics yield a flood of proofs of the historical accuracy and truth of the text of Holy Scripture. And the truth and validity of the text of course include all of God's miraculous interventions too. For the Gospels, written as they are, would have been the worst possible recipe for spreading a new faith if they were not true and completely reliable. Because, written very soon after the events, they describe very specific things on the one hand, and on the other they call on a great many witnesses (for example, the raising of the widow's son at Nain, the day of Pentecost and so on)

All this evidence of course serves to complement the wider reliability of Holy Scripture, which a person perceives and experiences when they come to know Jesus Christ and taste God's work in their life.
