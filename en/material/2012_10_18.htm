Recent developments on climate change

=============================================== ◄ Recent developments on climate change ► ===============================================

A great number of important developments about climate change and its effects have come to public attention in recent years:

  • The rise in temperature worldwide is worrying scientists more and more. Indeed, as regards the land of the northern hemisphere, July 2012 was the hottest ever recorded.
  • A worrying decrease in the amount of ice in the Arctic. A typical example is the break-up of the Petermann Glacier in Greenland in 2010.

Some important consequences of these changes are:

– Even more of the Sun's heat being retained, since the ice that once reflected it is now turning into liquid water that absorbs it.

– Depletion of drinking water reserves, since ice and snow replenish large aquifers.

– A rise in sea level because continental glaciers are melting, resulting in floods, extreme weather and destabilised climate conditions.

– The release of microbes trapped in the ice, which on the one hand have never been studied for their possible effects on man, and on the other may cause methane to be produced, so speeding up climate change.

– The release of huge amounts of carbon dioxide that are thought to be locked in the ice.

Further, from other news we also learn that:

  • Despite the constant increase in carbon dioxide emissions, the oceans and the land ecosystems continue to act as regulating mechanisms, absorbing about half of the carbon dioxide emitted. But at some point this capacity to absorb will reach saturation point. There is also always the danger that all this stored amount will be released, perhaps even all at once.
  • Air pollution is one more candidate for the number one cause of death in the coming decades.
  • Recent research analysing data on global temperatures over the last decades links the recent episodes of extremely high summer temperatures (heatwaves) to climate change.
  • The thinning of the ozone layer is beginning to take on worrying proportions, since in recent years a second ozone hole, this time over the Arctic, has also been discovered.
  • Fears are being expressed that climate change may endanger international peace, because of unrest and the migration of populations whose living conditions are changing.

All this shows, as some researchers comment, that we are entering a new climate regime. Indeed, it becomes clear that because of the interdependence of the natural climate mechanisms, any disturbance of one of them affects the working of the others, so causing a chain reaction.

So the endurance of the climate's regulating mechanisms is being tested to its limits, since despite all the bad news and warnings, governments do not seem willing to take drastic measures. Indeed, for many climate change is also a source of business profit , since the melting of the ice in regions such as Greenland is revealing rich mineral deposits.

Appendix 1: “A brief description of the greenhouse effect”

Let us recall that the greenhouse effect is in the first place a natural phenomenon that helps to create the right conditions for life on our planet. Specifically, thanks to this effect the average temperature of the Earth is 15°C; otherwise it would be -18°C. It works by trapping part of the heat that comes from the Sun so that it is not reflected back into space. This trapping is done by certain gases in the atmosphere, mainly carbon dioxide (CO2) and water vapour, which absorb part of the heat and re-emit it towards the Earth's surface, just as happens in real greenhouses, where the heat is held in by the glass. Various factors (mainly human but also natural) can cause an increase in the “greenhouse gases” mentioned above, so that more heat is retained, which leads to a rise in the planet's temperature.

Appendix 2: “A few words about the ozone hole”

As for ozone, let us recall that it is a gas found in an upper part of the atmosphere (the stratosphere), and since it forms a layer there, it is able to “filter” the incoming solar radiation, absorbing a large part of the ultraviolet radiation, which is dangerous to the health of living organisms. But the reckless use of chlorofluorocarbons in past decades in air conditioners and refrigerators, and as propellants in sprays, released chlorine radicals, which on reaching the stratosphere cause extensive breakdown of ozone, so that the protective layer is weakened and more ultraviolet radiation gets through. At the end of the 1980s it was discovered that over the Antarctic the ozone layer had become so thin that a “hole” had formed.
