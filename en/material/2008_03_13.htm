The bibliographical reliability of the New Testament

The reliability of the New Testament is now well established from the bibliographical side, that is, through the study of the surviving manuscript copies of the original text.

The manuscripts of the first writers, the originals, do not survive today; this is true of every text from antiquity. But we do have fragments and also complete codices, that is, collections of manuscripts containing the whole text of the New Testament.

The fragments that survive today are earlier than the codices and date from the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD. The main papyrus collections containing fragments of the New Testament are the Bodmer papyri and the Chester Beatty papyri. But there are even older fragments; the oldest dates from AD 130 or earlier. It belongs to the John Rylands papyrus collection and is a fragment of the 18th chapter of the Gospel of John. The codices, as we said, are collections of manuscripts containing the complete text of the New Testament. The oldest are the Codex Sinaiticus and the Codex Vaticanus, which date from the early 4th century AD.

On the basis of the surviving fragments and codices, every text from antiquity is subjected to what is called textual criticism. This is a set of methods for examining how faithful a text is to the original. Textual criticism comes down to the following four questions:

1. How many copies exist?

Why it matters: the more copies there are, the more reliably we can reconstruct the original text.

Answer:
For reliable ancient Greek works, such as those of Sophocles and Thucydides, there are as few as 10 copies or fewer. For the Iliad there are 643 manuscripts. For the New Testament there are more than 24,000! (of which 5,300 copies are written in Greek). And if we add the passages quoted in the writings of the Church Fathers, we have a total of about 200,000 references.

2. How much time separates the original texts from the oldest known copies?

Why it matters: the shorter the time between the copy and the original, the more reliable the copy is considered.

Answer:
For ancient works like those mentioned in the previous question, the time separating the original text from its oldest known copy ranges from 700 to 1,800 years. For the New Testament the corresponding gap is estimated at only 50 to 200 years!

3. Over what geographical area were the copies found?

Why it matters: copies spread over a wide geographical area allow us to compare them region by region, so that changes to the text introduced locally can be identified.

Answer:
Copies of the New Testament have been found in Egypt, Palestine, Syria, Turkey, Italy and a few in Greece. On the differences between them, see the next question.

4. What differences exist between the surviving copies, and of what kind?

Why it matters: the fewer the differences between the copies, the more reliably we can reconstruct the original text.

Answer:
Mistakes in copying are unavoidable, not necessarily deliberate, but because of the human factor and the conditions under which copying was done. For comparison, 5% of the text of Homer's Iliad is in doubt, while for the New Testament only 0.5% is in doubt: 40 lines out of 20,000. In some codices small passages are missing, but they do not affect matters of doctrine or teaching.

For more detailed information, please see the related material in the right-hand column.

The conclusion is that the texts of the New Testament pass every test of reliability, and of authenticity as well, since they were written and widely circulated within the same generation in which the events they describe took place; so if things had been otherwise, they could very easily have been refuted by eyewitnesses who were still alive. What is more, the care taken from the start to preserve them for the future shows respect for the genuineness of the text.

There is also a great number of passages from the New Testament quoted word for word in the writings of the Apostolic Fathers of the first three centuries. There are so many of these quotations that the whole New Testament (except for 11 verses) could be reconstructed from them alone! This shows that the New Testament existed in written form early on (at the end of the 1st century), and that people believed it their duty to preserve the texts exactly as they had received them from the original writers.

At the Council of Carthage in AD 397 the books that make up the New Testament today were finally settled; that is, the canon of the New Testament took shape. The choice of these 27 books was not the result of a meeting of a closed circle of clergy who shaped the canon of the New Testament according to the beliefs or interests of a ruling minority. The choice of the 27 books of the New Testament was the confirmation and ratification of texts that the church and the Christian community had already accepted and taken to heart. So we are not talking about deciding which books were sacred, but simply about making the canon of the New Testament official. The criteria for choosing these 27 books, which ensured that they were given by inspiration of God, were:

  • That they were written by an apostle or by a man closely connected
    with an apostle
  • The spiritual quality of their content
  • Their acceptance by the Christian churches as a whole

It is also remarkable that the texts of the New Testament were translated into many languages, and fairly early on, which helped the texts to spread and made the New Testament accessible to ordinary people. The spread and use of the Greek language also played an important part, since it can express meaning with clarity, precision and relative simplicity.

All this evidence obliges anyone who questions the reliability of the New Testament texts to reject the whole of ancient Greek and Latin literature as well, since the evidence on which its reliability rests is very slight compared with the mass of evidence that confirms the reliability of the New Testament.

So we see that the Lord, in a wonderful way, took care to preserve His written Word through the centuries and to bring it safely into our hands today.
