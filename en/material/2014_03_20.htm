The cell: the structural and functional unit of life

All organisms are made of cells. This statement sums up the cell theory, first put forward in 1838–39 by M. Schleiden (M. Schleiden) and Th. Schwann (Th. Schwann), according to which “the fundamental structural and functional unit of all organisms is the cell”. This means that the cell is the smallest structure in nature in which the phenomenon of life appears.

Moreover, in 1885 R. Virchow (R. Virchow) put forward the principle that “every cell comes from a cell”. This principle is especially important, since indeed it has never been observed in nature, nor produced under laboratory conditions: the creation of a cell from non-living matter, as the theory of evolution supposes happened, and by chance at that. Indeed, related experiments, such as those of Miller–Urey (1953), which tried to recreate the conditions (the primordial soup) under which the first cell was supposedly formed by chance, give negative results to this day. On the contrary, as early as 1861 (that is, just 2 years after the first publication of Charles Darwin's “Origin of Species”), Louis Pasteur, through a series of experiments, proved that the microbes and insects seen, for example, on spoiled meat are not born spontaneously from dead matter, but come from eggs laid on the meat earlier by various insects or micro-organisms. So the principle of biogenesis was stated: “all life comes from life”.

As for their structure, cells can be divided into two great categories: prokaryotic cells, which are simpler and in which the genetic material (DNA) is not inside a nucleus (for example bacteria), and eukaryotic cells, which are more complex and have a nucleus in which the genetic material is found. Plant, animal and all many-celled organisms are made of eukaryotic cells.

Although in their pictures in books cells give the impression of flat, static structures, in reality they are three-dimensional structures, bustling with activity, as we will see below. Also, cells are not all the same. In man there are about 100 different kinds of cell, each with its own characteristic form, each carrying out a particular function (for example contraction: muscle cells; passing on messages: nerve cells; covering: epithelial cells and so on).

The shape of cells is such that they have a small volume and at the same time the largest possible surface for that volume. So they meet two conflicting requirements at once: a large surface for easy exchange of substances and messages, and a small volume for more efficient working and faster cooperation of their organelles.

The cell is separated from its outside environment by the plasma membrane, which works like a fence – but one that, while stable, is not static; on the contrary, its structural units give it fluidity, making it dynamic, so that it recognises and allows the selective passage of substances, and also exchanges messages (communication with other cells). If the plasma membrane were an impermeable envelope, the cell could neither feed nor secrete substances. If, on the other hand, it let every chemical through, the chemical make-up of the cell could not be maintained (diffusion). But it is wonderful that the plasma membrane is selectively permeable and is in effect an intelligent border between the cell and its environment.

As for the inside of the cell, simple observation under the microscope shows the cell as a uniform, semi-fluid mass in which the nucleus can be seen. That is why in earlier times this semi-fluid mass (“protoplasm”) was believed to be the basic substance of living matter (that is, something like a magical living substance with no inner structure).

Today, with the help of the electron microscope and modern methods of biochemical analysis, we know that the inside of the cell is indeed liquid (the cytoplasm), but within it there are a great number of organelles, placed in particular positions (the cytoskeleton), each carrying out a particular function, obeying the “commands” that come from the cell nucleus. Here are some examples:

  • Nucleus: it keeps the genetic material (DNA). On the basis of the information recorded in it, the properties of the cell, and so of the organism, are determined, and all the cell's activities are controlled. It is also in the nucleus that the genetic material is duplicated, in a way that ensures that genetic information is passed on unchanged from cell to cell and from generation to generation. Finally, in the nucleus the various kinds of RNA are made from genetic information carried by the DNA.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum: a complex system of channels and sacs made of membranes. It works as a channel for transporting substances. It is divided into the rough endoplasmic reticulum (which has ribosomes and carries out protein synthesis) and the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (making lipids, neutralising toxic substances).
  • Golgi apparatus: a system of membranes in which proteins are processed.
  • Mitochondria (in all cells) & chloroplasts (only in plant cells): organelles that convert the energy the cell receives from its environment into a usable form. They are also called the “power stations” of cells. It is worth mentioning here that energy is carried to the various parts of the cell by adenosine triphosphate (ATP), a chemical compound that acts as the go-between in the dealings between the cell processes that release energy and those that use it, which is why it is called the “energy currency” or the “rechargeable battery”: to give up its energy it is broken down (discharged) and then, through special processes, it is put together again (recharged).
  • Lysosomes: they help the cell to digest large molecules and also micro-organisms that may have invaded the cell.
  • Peroxisomes: they contain oxidising enzymes that carry out various metabolic processes. In the peroxisomes of liver and kidney cells in particular, the body is detoxified of any alcohol that has been consumed.

From the above it becomes clear that a structure such as the cell cannot be the product of random chemical processes. It is not a chaotically complex system that formed by chance and began to work so smoothly. And it is a fact that in living organisms we meet a complexity that is not chaotic (it would then have no functional use) but specified. And a complexity that is specified cannot come about by chance; it needs an intelligent designer to determine the function of each part so that the result as a whole is harmonious and works.

From a mathematical point of view, moreover, if all the chemical processes required were left to take place entirely at random, even under the most favourable possible conditions, the probability of forming a single living molecule is of the order of 1 in 1060! Indeed, the well-known scientist Fred Hoyle (1915–2001) admitted that accepting the chance creation of even the simplest cell is like claiming that a tornado sweeping through a scrapyard assembled a Boeing 747 by chance, while the possibility of even one working protein forming as a chance combination of amino acids is like a whole solar system full of blind people all solving a Rubik's cube at the same time. In other words, mathematics makes the possibility of life arising by chance impossible!

Moreover, the fact that science has been able to look inside the cell and reveal the complexity of its structure and of the processes that take place within it is a serious blow to the theory of evolution, since it is impossible for even the simplest cell to have come about by chance. So the inconsistency of the theory of evolution is not limited to its inability to explain how one species evolved into another, but extends to the level of how the first cell came into being. In other words, the theory of evolution is already called into question at the level of the fundamental unit of life!

And He who is the Creator of all things can also become the personal Saviour of every person who believes in Him, for He “was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification” (Romans 4:25)
