The Bible and the Greek language (Part 1)

Translated from the Greek original in the newspaper “Christianity”. Read it in Greek

April 2020 · The “Christianity & Science” team

“And this gospel of the kingdom shall be preached in all the world for a witness unto all nations; and then shall the end come.” (Matthew 24:14)

As these lines are written, a heavy veil of fear and insecurity weighs down on the planet. First came the destructive wildfires, then the earthquakes, and then followed the Covid-19 pestilence (an infectious disease), which as a pandemic is now striking people’s health and the structures of states, with thousands of victims.

The only truly comforting and hopeful message in the whole world is the message of the Gospel – the heavenly message of God’s love: “[…] God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.” (John 3:16). A powerful and completely true message!

The Lord of the harvest, the Holy Spirit, is stirring the hearts of believers, on the one hand to proclaim the joyful message of the Kingdom of God, and on the other for all of us to get ready to meet the Lord. “And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world. Amen.” (Matthew 28:18–20)

In spreading the message of the Gospel, Greece has a very special part… and a responsibility too. More than 300 years before the birth of the Saviour Jesus Christ, Alexander the Great spread the Greek language throughout the known world of the time, and he is referred to precisely in the book of the prophet Daniel (581 BC) as the first king of Greece (Daniel 8:21)!

The Old Testament was written in Hebrew, but it was very soon translated into Greek. Ptolemy Philadelphus, one of the successors of Alexander the Great, arranged for the Old Testament to be translated from Hebrew into Greek, a language that even the Jews of the dispersion understood better. The translation was made by 72 scholars (known as the “translation of the Seventy”, the Septuagint, for ease of remembering) around 280 BC in Alexandria in Egypt.

The New Testament was written from the start, entirely in Greek! It is truly a great honour that God used the Greek language to convey to people both His plans and His character.

Why Greek?

Although some dispute it, Greek is the richest language in the world, with five million words and 70 million written forms.

Greek has words for ideas that have no equivalent in other languages, such as amilla (honourable rivalry), thalpori (cosy warmth) and philotimo (a sense of honour and generosity). Only Greek distinguishes life as such (zoi) from a person’s course of life (vios), and love (agapi) from passionate love (eros). Only Greek, keeping the same root, distinguishes an accident (atychima) from a fatal accident (dystychima), or self-interest (symferon) from interest in something (endiaferon).

In every language we have the signifier (the word) and the signified (the meaning). In Greek these two have an original relationship, because, unlike other languages, the signifier is not a random string of letters.

In an ordinary language like English we could all agree to call a cloud a “car” and a car a “cloud”, and from the moment we agreed it would hold. In Greek there is no need for such a thing. The car is an aftokinito (afto + kinito: the object that moves by itself!).

That is why many people distinguish Greek as a “conceptual” language from the other, “semiological” languages.

For example, fthonos (envy) comes from the verb fthino, which means to diminish. And indeed envy, as a feeling, slowly diminishes and destroys us. It “diminishes” us as people, and wears down even our health. And of course, when we speak of something there is so much of that it never runs out, what do we call it? Why, afthono – “abundant”, literally “undiminishing”.

It is a truly moving feeling to speak and at the same time to realise exactly what you are saying – to utter each word, the signifier, and at the same time to think of its meaning, the signified.

Our country, and the whole world, is going through a very difficult time. Let us ask the Lord to give us sincere repentance, and let us cry out to Him from the heart. The Lord will have compassion on us and help us! After all, voithos (helper) means the one who runs when called: voi = a cry + theo = to run.

“…this is good and acceptable in the sight of God our Saviour; who will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth. For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; who gave himself a ransom for all, to be testified in due time.” (1 Timothy 2:3–6)
