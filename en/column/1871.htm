The bibliographical reliability of the New Testament (Part 3)

Translated from the Greek original in the newspaper “Christianity”. Read it in Greek

March 2019 · The “Christianity & Science” team

In our previous feature we looked at the archaeological and historical reliability of the New Testament. In this article we want to let our readers know that the reliability of the New Testament is also established bibliographically – that is, through the study of the surviving manuscript copies of the original text.

The manuscripts of the first authors – the originals – do not survive today, which is true of all the texts of antiquity. But we have in our hands fragments and also complete codices, that is, collections of manuscripts containing the whole text of the New Testament.

The fragments that survive today are earlier than the codices and date from the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD. The main collections of papyri containing fragments of the New Testament are the Bodmer papyri and the Chester Beatty papyri.

But there are also older fragments, the oldest dated to AD 130 or even earlier. It belongs to the John Rylands collection of papyri and is a fragment of the 18th chapter of the Gospel of John. As for the codices, as we said, these are collections of manuscripts containing the complete text of the New Testament. The oldest are the Codex Sinaiticus and the Codex Vaticanus, dated to the early 4th century AD.

On the basis of the surviving fragments and codices, every ancient text is subjected to what is called textual criticism: a set of methods for examining how far a text is a reliable witness to the original. Textual criticism can be summed up in the following four questions:

  1. How many copies exist?

Why it matters: The more copies there are, the more reliably we can reconstruct the original text.

Answer: For reliable ancient Greek writings, such as works of Sophocles and Thucydides, there are fewer than 10 copies. For the Iliad there are 643 manuscripts. For the New Testament there are more than 24,000! (of which 5,300 are written in Greek). And if we add the quotations of passages in the writings of the church fathers, we have a total of about 200,000 references!

  1. How much time separates the original texts from the oldest known copies?

Why it matters: The shorter the time between the copy and the original, the more reliable the copy is considered.

Answer: For ancient writings like those mentioned in the previous question, the time between the original text and its oldest copy found ranges from 700 to 1,800 years. For the New Testament, the corresponding gap is estimated at only 50 to 200 years!

  1. Over what geographical area are the copies found?

Why it matters: Copies spread over a wide geographical area let us compare them region by region, to show up local differences introduced into the text.

Answer: Copies of the New Testament have been found in Egypt, Palestine, Syria, Turkey, Italy and some in Greece. As for the differences between them, see the next question.

  1. What differences are there between the surviving copies, and of what kind?

Why it matters: The fewer the differences between the copies, the more reliably we can reconstruct the original text.

Answer: Errors in copying are unavoidable – not necessarily deliberate, but because of the human factor and the conditions under which copying was done. For example, in Homer’s Iliad 5% of the text is in question, whereas in the New Testament only 0.5% is in question – specifically 40 lines out of 20,000. In some codices small passages are missing, but these do not affect matters of doctrine or teaching.

The conclusion is that the texts of the New Testament pass every test of reliability and also of authenticity, since they were written and widely circulated within the same generation in which the events they describe took place. So if reality had been different, they would very easily have been contradicted by the eyewitnesses who were still alive. What is more, the care taken from the beginning to preserve them for the future shows respect for the genuineness of the text.
