Our column in the newspaper “Christianity”

Resurrection or digital immortality?

Translated from the Greek original in the newspaper “Christianity”. Read it in Greek

September 2026 · The “Christianity & Science” team

“Can we expect from technology what we once hoped for from religion? ‘Death-bots’ are digital likenesses built from the material left behind by loved ones who have died – voice messages, talks, writings, emails – and, thanks to artificial intelligence, they reproduce those people as they talk with the living.

Many people, however, need ‘grief-bots’: digital likenesses of the recently departed that go on talking with the living until the trauma of parting has been overcome. Seventeen-year-old Joaquin Oliver, killed in a school shooting in Florida in 2018, was ‘brought back’ so that his parents could hear his voice again. Alexis Ohanian, co-founder of Reddit, built a digital likeness of his mother so that he could feel her embrace again […] Parents who have died can […] ‘enjoy’ a grandchild they never lived to see. The sages of antiquity can advise us about our troubles…”

This passage, from the article “Resurrection or digital immortality?” in the newspaper To Vima of 11 April 2026, gives us the opportunity to look at the difference between two completely different approaches to death: the technological attempt to prolong a person’s presence through artificial intelligence, and the Christian hope of a real resurrection.

First of all, using technology for such purposes reveals something very deep: that man does not come to terms with death. His desire to see his loved ones again, to hear them and talk with them, is so strong that today people are trying to satisfy it through digital replicas.

However impressive these so-called death-bots or grief-bots may be, they are not a return of a person to life. They are nothing more than complex algorithms that imitate the way a person expressed himself, based on the data that has been collected. There is no real communication, and no relationship with the person who died. He is still dead, and what talks with the living is nothing more than a simulation.

The wish for the dead to “enjoy” something they did not live to see, or to go on talking with their relatives, is moving; but at the same time it shows how inadequate the technological solution is. A piece of software can produce words, but it cannot create real presence, real love or a real embrace.

By contrast, as we shall see, the promise of the Resurrection concerns the whole person, for God does not promise merely to keep some information about a person, but to give him real incorruption.

A little further on, the same article says:

“The technological vision of immortality is mainly about uploading the data of consciousness to a computer. It rests on the assumption … that brain signals can be scanned with full ability to decode and digitise them, so that through reverse engineering we can model human consciousness, until we have a back-up of every human being.”

Without dismissing or belittling modern technological capabilities, we want at this point to stress the fundamental difference between the Christian faith and the idea that a human being is essentially the information his brain contains, so that if this is transferred to a computer, the person “will go on existing”.

The Bible presents man as a person created in the image of God. His personality is not simply data, memories or neural signals, and that is why the hope of salvation is not the copying of consciousness but the resurrection of the person himself by his Creator.

The Word of God, in other words, does not promise a digital imitation, or memories preserved on some computer, but the resurrection of the person himself.

“the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, and shall come forth” (John 5:28–29)

They will not come forth from some data server or from a database, but from the graves. When Jesus raised Lazarus, He did not comfort Martha by giving her a memory of her brother, or an illusion of his presence, but said to her: “Thy brother shall rise again” (John 11:23).

The Christian hope, then, is not the preservation of an image of a person but the restoration of his very life.

The “Christianity & Science” team
