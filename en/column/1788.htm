Cloning: the sequel

Translated from the Greek original in the newspaper “Christianity”. Read it in Greek

April 2018 · The “Christianity & Science” team

“All things are lawful unto me, but all things are not expedient” (1 Corinthians 6:12)

At the beginning of the year the world scientific community was astonished by the announcement from the Institute of Neuroscience of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai that two macaque monkeys had been cloned for the first time. The importance of the achievement lies in the fact that for the first time a primate – an animal that in terms of its body has many similarities with man – has been cloned. According to the researchers themselves, it took 79 failed attempts before the scientists succeeded, that is, before they had two viable clones without obvious problems.
The Chinese researchers stated that they do not intend to pursue the cloning of humans, but that the cloned monkeys, being genetically identical, will be used in research into diseases that affect humans, such as cancer. But other scientists are already expressing concern that, since their colleagues in Shanghai have managed to “break” the barrier of the technical difficulties of cloning primates, the way is inevitably opening to the creation of human clones (reproductive cloning).

The subject of cloning began to take on great importance after the birth of the famous sheep Dolly in 1997 in Edinburgh, Scotland, the first mammal cloned by implanting into an egg the nucleus of a cell from an adult animal. At this point we should remember that in the nucleus of every cell there is DNA, the genetic material in which all the information about the structure and working of the organism is encoded. So by implanting into an egg (whose own nucleus has first been removed) the nucleus of a cell from another organism (containing that organism’s DNA), an embryo can be created that is genetically identical to that organism (a clone). We have written at more length about the history and techniques of cloning in earlier features (Christianity, May, June and July 2002). Since then many other mammals have been cloned, such as cows, pigs, rabbits, dogs, cats and rodents.

The truth is that things are not as ideal as they are presented. For example, with cloning only two attempts in a hundred succeed, and every attempt involves the unavoidable destruction of dozens or hundreds of embryos that are judged unsuitable or fail to survive. Also, the cloned organism usually has serious pathological problems, while the whole procedure has dangerous side effects for the animals that carry the clone, too.

Trying to give reproductive cloning a romantic cover, one gynaecologist said that “every infertile couple should have the opportunity to have offspring”. But that is not right. In normal conception and birth, half the chromosomes come from the father and half from the mother, so the child is born of the couple. In cloning, all the chromosomes come from the person seeking to produce an exact copy of himself. For this reason reproductive cloning is not a cure for a couple’s infertility but a sacrifice on the altar of human narcissism and self-satisfaction. And we say it is a sacrifice because the whole process is bloodstained. Specifically, 400 eggs are needed, taken from about 40 women donors (each gives up to 15 eggs) who have been given fertility drugs. The nucleus is removed from each egg, and it is placed next to a body cell of the person to be cloned. Those cells that are activated and begin to divide are placed as embryos in the wombs of women willing to act as surrogate mothers. Most of the embryos are either miscarried or aborted when they are found to have serious congenital abnormalities. It is estimated that about 50 women would need to carry a pregnancy for one clone to be born, who may be healthy, or may have serious abnormalities that appear either at once or later in life.

In view of all this, it is no surprise that bioethics committees in every country, including Greece, recommend a strict ban on reproductive cloning.
The consequences of applying cloning to humans are therefore obvious: the repeated attempts, with the destruction of embryos that follows, would completely devalue human existence, making man an expendable laboratory animal that is copied and destroyed without hindrance until the “desired result” is achieved.

But to God the Father every person is unique and precious, and it was this love that led the Son of God to the sacrifice of the cross, to give man His “unspeakable gift” (2 Corinthians 9:15).
“For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life” (John 3:16).
“For our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ: who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body, according to the working whereby he is able even to subdue all things unto himself.” (Philippians 3:20–21)
