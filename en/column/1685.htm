“From whence cometh my help?” (Psalm 121:1)

On 14 and 15 July the uncrewed spacecraft New Horizons came closest (12,000 km) to the dwarf planet Pluto, after a journey of about nine years and 4.8 billion kilometres. The probe sent back to Earth the closest and clearest photographs of Pluto ever taken, from which valuable information will be gained about this heavenly body at the edge of our solar system, being explored for the first time.

Last November the robotic lander Philae became the first spacecraft to land on a comet. After about seven months of “silence” because of technical problems, Philae “woke up” and began sending data to Earth. But on 9 July it fell silent again. One of the main aims of the mission is to find out whether there are complex organic molecules related to life on the surface of comets.

Also, on 20 July at the Royal Society in London, the Breakthrough Listen project was announced: an ambitious new programme to search for advanced extraterrestrial civilisations, using machines of very great computing power that will receive and process huge volumes of data from the world’s largest radio telescopes. The famous astrophysicist Stephen Hawking, keynote speaker at the announcement, said that the new programme “raises the hopes of answering the question whether man has company in the universe. Man has a deep need to explore, to learn, to know; he is a social being, so it is important for us to know whether we are alone in the universe.”

It is clear that in our days, more than ever, there is an anxious effort to reach out into space. Ambitious plans are drawn up, important missions are carried out, large sums are spent and new reports keep arriving about the possibility that space may become either a place of escape from Earth’s problems or a place from which some kind of salvation, or a new beginning for humanity, will come through contact with extraterrestrial civilisations (techno-animism).

The Word of God says: “Thy wisdom and thy knowledge, it hath perverted thee” (Isaiah 47:10). And this is not because the scientific studies and space explorations being carried out are not truly valuable, but because the very science that urges humanity to turn its hopes to space is the same science that shows us how utopian those hopes are.

Today almost half the Earth’s population is convinced that there is some kind of intelligent extraterrestrial life somewhere in the vast universe, and that interstellar travel is a relatively easy matter that will be achieved in time. But scientific studies, as we have seen in earlier features, show on the one hand that interstellar travel is limited by the laws of physics themselves, and on the other that it is mathematically and physically impossible for the conditions necessary for life to exist together anywhere else in the universe (the anthropic cosmological principle).

So these achievements urge humanity, in vain, to turn its gaze to the cold, dark, inhospitable reaches of space in search of answers and solutions. But the Word of God urges every person to lift his eyes to heaven, seeing it as the throne of the One from whom all true and real solutions come – and above all the solution to the problem of eternal death, since Jesus Christ came down from heaven (John 6:38) to find sinful man and pay the penalty of his sin by His sacrifice on the cross. And then, after He rose again, He “passed into the heavens” (Hebrews 4:14) and intercedes for us at the right hand of the Father (Hebrews 7:25). From heaven, too, comes the gift of the Holy Spirit (1 Peter 1:12), and the answer to every prayer (Psalm 20:6). Finally, from heaven Jesus Christ will come to receive His church (1 Thessalonians 4:16).

So indeed help and salvation come from heaven – not through scientific discoveries or from some extraterrestrials, but from God the Father through Jesus Christ:

“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights” (James 1:17)

The “Christianity & Science” team
