The benefits of delayed gratification (Part 1)

Translated from the Greek original in the newspaper “Christianity”. Read it in Greek

June 2023 · The “Christianity & Science” team

“By faith Moses, when he was come to years, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter; choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season; esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt: for he had respect unto the recompence of the reward.” (Hebrews 11:24–26)

In 1972 a very interesting experiment was carried out at Stanford University by the psychologist Walter Mischel. He wanted to study how far young children are willing to resist instant gratification in order to win greater pleasure later, and what each child’s reaction might reveal about the later course of his life. The experiment is known as “The Marshmallow Experiment” and was carried out like this: a child aged 4–6 is offered a marshmallow by a kindly teacher, with the choice either to eat it straight away or to resist the desire for instant gratification for 15 minutes, while the teacher is out of the room – with the prize of another marshmallow when the teacher returns, so that the reward is doubled. This test was carried out with 600 children. While each child waited alone, the researchers watched their reactions with a hidden camera. These can be sorted into the following four groups:

1. Successful resistance to the desire to eat the marshmallow, by trying to avoid looking at it and keeping busy with some other object in the room. In this way there was no displeasure or distress during the wait.

2. Also a successful outcome, but with great effort and struggle on the child’s part to resist, which could be seen from the nervousness of his reactions.

3. The child trying to benefit straight away, but only so far as it would not be noticed (for example, with little bites or by licking the marshmallow), so as not to lose the prize at the end.

4. Eating the marshmallow straight away, without any attempt to resist the desire for instant gratification.

The conclusion was that, broadly speaking, each child’s reaction was an indication of the later course of his life. The children who managed to hold back in the experiment later had better school and academic results, a lower body mass index, lower rates of substance addiction and also lower divorce rates in their families, and in general showed more patience and determination in reaching their goals in adult life.

The researchers stressed, of course, that this does not mean that virtues such as patience and self-control are inborn, so that everyone else is condemned to give in to temptation and not make progress. On the contrary, self-control can be taught, and the abilities that help us delay gratification and be patient are the same ones that help us make better choices in life without being drawn away by harmful temptations. The most important factor shaping a child’s behaviour in such situations is thought to be the family environment – more specifically, the examples children see in their parents from a very early age (2–3 years) in how they react to similar temptations.

The second factor found to be at work is the way the goal is presented. Simply put, for a child to be convinced that it is worth being patient, he must know that the promised reward is real, specific (not vague) and acceptable to him (that is, something he likes).

From all this we can clearly see, on the one hand, the points in common with the Christian fight and the patience it requires, and on the other, how the scientific conclusions correspond to the spiritual guidance the Word of God gives. We will speak about these, God willing, in our next feature.
