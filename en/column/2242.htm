The benefits of delayed gratification (Part 2)

Translated from the Greek original in the newspaper “Christianity”. Read it in Greek

July 2023 · The “Christianity & Science” team

“For ye have need of patience, that, after ye have done the will of God, ye might receive the promise.” (Hebrews 10:36)

In our previous feature we described the so-called “Marshmallow experiment”, which studied how young children reacted when asked not to eat a sweet in front of them straight away, but to hold back for a while, with another sweet as the reward. At the end of the experiment it was found that some children resisted successfully, waiting patiently for their reward; others resisted, but with great effort; some tried to eat little bits so as not to be noticed and not lose the prize; and some could not resist at all and ate the sweet at once, not caring about the future reward.

Although the researchers found that the children’s reactions were an indication of the later course of their lives, they stressed that virtues such as patience and self-control are not fixed in advance in each person, but can be cultivated and developed.

The similarities between these reactions, and the conclusions drawn from them, and the “good fight of faith” (1 Timothy 6:12) that every committed Christian is called to fight are remarkable. On one side there is a host of offers that easily provide instant satisfaction of all kinds of desires that stir the human heart; on the other there is the Lord’s call to self-control and self-denial, in order to win the highest goal at stake, which is nothing less than the salvation of the soul and the kingdom of heaven.

We might perhaps match types of Christians, or of people in general, with the four kinds of reaction of the children in the experiment. Starting from the end, we could say that there really are people, perhaps even Christians, whose complete lack of self-control makes them slaves to the need for instant satisfaction of a desire; or who struggle to keep their balance, wavering between the pleasures of the world and the kingdom of heaven, walking at the “edges” of the will of God. There are also Christians who sincerely want to do the will of God, but the influences on them still have great power, so that they suffer and find it very hard to do the will of God.

The healthy group, to which the Lord wants us to rise, is the one in which our spiritual reserves and the strength that comes from the grace of God help us to pass by temptations, remaining steadfast and whole.

“But the fruit of the Spirit is […] temperance” (Galatians 5:22–23)

Being filled with the Holy Spirit, and the certainties that spring from the Word of God, train the Christian and lead him in the right direction. For he is informed and assured that the patience and resistance the Lord asks him to show in the face of temptations to instant satisfaction – or even, often, unanswered requests in prayer – are not meant to toughen him through a feeling of deprivation. On the contrary, they guarantee a higher reward that will come at the right time in every area of life: from marriage, a career, the outcome of a problem or an illness, to the highest goal of all, which is entering the kingdom of heaven. At the same time, God’s presence in his life leaves no room for a feeling of lack and emptiness, and is a real source of strength in this fight.

“But if we hope for that we see not, then do we with patience wait for it.” (Romans 8:25)

So the call of the Gospel is not to a miserable life full of feelings of deprivation, but to the self-control and patience that befit the wealth of blessings God has prepared for His children:

“Wherefore gird up the loins of your mind, be sober, and hope to the end for the grace that is to be brought unto you at the revelation of Jesus Christ” (1 Peter 1:13)
